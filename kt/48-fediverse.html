Fediverse and AT Protocol

Two open social networks Airhop could bridge into, planned for v1.2.0 and not implemented. This chapter explains what they actually are, how an integration would work, what it would cost in privacy, and my honest view on whether it is worth doing.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Instead of one giant website that everyone must join, imagine thousands of small clubs that all know how to pass notes to each other. You join one club, and you can still talk to people in all the others. That is the Fediverse, and Mastodon is the most popular kind of club. Bluesky works differently: there is one shared filing cabinet everyone reads from, but you own your own drawer and can move it. Airhop could post into either, but the moment it does, whatever it posts is public and permanent in a way a Bluetooth message never is.

Three protocols, three shapes

Airhop already speaks one decentralised protocol. Understanding why Nostr was chosen over these two explains most of the integration question.

The decision log gives the reasoning directly: Matrix and XMPP were rejected because they require an always-on server, which is a single point of failure; Signal was rejected because it owns the infrastructure and requires a phone number. ActivityPub has the same server dependency as Matrix. Nostr was chosen for permissionless keypair identity, hundreds of independent relays, and because bitchat had already validated it.

How ActivityPub works

A W3C standard from 2018. The Fediverse is the network of servers speaking it: Mastodon, Pleroma, Misskey, PeerTube, Pixelfed, Lemmy and others. 

  THE FOUR CONCEPTS

  ACTOR      A person or a bot, as a JSON-LD document at a URL.
             Carries: inbox URL, outbox URL, a public key, a display name.

  OBJECT     A thing. Note (a post), Image, Video, Article, Person.

  ACTIVITY   A verb wrapping an object.
             Create, Follow, Like, Announce (boost), Delete, Undo.

  INBOX      An HTTP endpoint. Delivery is a POST to it.
  OUTBOX     An HTTP endpoint listing what an actor has published.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PUBLISHING A POST

  1. Alice writes on mastodon.social
  2. Her server wraps it:
        { "type": "Create",
          "actor": "https://mastodon.social/users/alice",
          "object": { "type": "Note", "content": "hello" },
          "to": ["https://www.w3.org/ns/activitystreams#Public"] }
  3. Her server looks up every FOLLOWER's inbox
  4. It POSTs the activity to each one, signed with an HTTP Signature
     using her server-held key
  5. Each receiving server stores it and shows it to its own users

  DISCOVERY

  WebFinger:  GET /.well-known/webfinger?resource=acct:alice@mastodon.social
              -> the URL of Alice's actor document
PropertyConsequence
Push deliveryYour server actively POSTs to thousands of follower inboxes. That is real infrastructure with real cost, which is why running a Mastodon instance is work.
The server holds your keyHTTP Signatures are made by the server, not by you. Your instance can post as you. This is the single most important fact for our purposes.
Identity is server-bound@alice@mastodon.social. If that server closes, the identity is gone. Migration exists and is partial: followers move, posts largely do not.
"Direct" is not privateA direct message is a Note addressed to specific actors. Both servers store it in plaintext and both operators can read it. There is no end-to-end encryption in ActivityPub.
Deletion is a requestA Delete activity asks other servers to remove a copy. Compliance is voluntary.
ModerationPer-instance, by human admins, with instance-level blocks. Genuinely effective, and a completely different model from Airhop's client-side-only rule.

How AT Protocol works

Bluesky's protocol. Deliberately different from ActivityPub, and the differences are instructive. 

  THE FOUR COMPONENTS

  PDS      Personal Data Server. Holds YOUR repository: a signed,
           content-addressed Merkle tree of everything you have posted.
           You can self-host it or move it.

  DID      Decentralised Identifier. did:plc:... or did:web:...
           Your PERMANENT identity, independent of any server.
           A handle (alice.bsky.social) is just a human-readable
           pointer that resolves to it.

  RELAY    Crawls every PDS and produces one firehose of all events.
           (Not a Nostr relay. Same word, opposite meaning: this one
           aggregates, Nostr's forwards.)

  APPVIEW  Consumes the firehose and builds the timelines, search,
           notifications and counts that a client actually reads.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  POSTING

  1. Alice's client signs a record and writes it to her PDS
  2. The PDS appends it to her signed Merkle repository
  3. The relay crawls it into the firehose
  4. Every AppView sees it and indexes it
  5. Followers' timelines are built by an AppView, not delivered by push
ActivityPubAT Protocol
DeliveryPush to inboxesPull from a firehose
Identity portabilityPartial. Followers move, posts largely do not.Full. The DID and the whole repo move.
Who signsThe serverYou. Records are signed into your repo.
ScalingEvery server must deliver to every followerLarge indexers do the heavy work
Practical decentralisationGenuinely many serversOne dominant relay and AppView today. Self-hosting a PDS is easy; running a competitive AppView is not.
ModerationPer instanceComposable labelling services, chosen by the user
Everything is publicMostlyStructurally. The firehose model means every post is crawled by design.

The relevant part for Airhop: records are signed by the user's own key, and identity is a DID that survives a server. That is much closer to Airhop's identity model than ActivityPub's server-holds-your-key arrangement.

The identity question

The roadmap's claim is that Airhop's identity model maps onto both, so bridging is an integration rather than a redesign. That is true for AT Protocol and only partly true for ActivityPub.

RequirementAirhop hasActivityPub needsAT Protocol needs
A signing keyEd25519An RSA or Ed25519 key, held by the serverA key you hold. did:key can be derived from Ed25519 directly.
A stable identifierPeer ID, Nostr pubkey@user@host, which requires a hostA DID, which does not
A place to publishNostr relaysA home serverA PDS, self-hostable
DiscoveryAnnounce, QR, geohashWebFingerDID resolution

The unavoidable answer to "is this account creation"

Yes. ActivityPub requires a home server that hosts your actor document and holds a key that posts as you. AT Protocol requires a PDS, which you can self-host but which is still a server that must exist and stay reachable.

Airhop's principle 3 is "no accounts, ever. There is no create-account screen." A bridge cannot honour that. What it can honour is principle 2, offline first, by being strictly optional and by leaving the mesh untouched when it is off.

So the honest framing is not "no accounts". It is: the app itself never requires an account, and a user who wants one for a specific purpose can attach it, knowing what it costs.

One further wrinkle: an Ed25519 key can be turned into a did:key directly, which is elegant. But did:plc, the DID method Bluesky actually uses in practice, is operated by Bluesky's own directory service. Using did:web or did:key is possible and less well supported. Deriving a DID from the Airhop identity is real, and it does not by itself remove the server.

What an integration would look like

Flow 1: cross-posting outward

  user writes in #bluetooth
        │
        ├── ALWAYS: signed packet 0x02, flooded over BLE
        │
        └── IF the bridge is enabled AND this message is opted in:
                 │
                 ├── ActivityPub: POST a Create/Note to the user's
                 │   outbox on their home server, which fans it out
                 │
                 └── AT Protocol: write a signed app.bsky.feed.post
                     record into the user's PDS repo

Why this must be per message, not per channel

A mesh room is ephemeral, local and mostly among people you can see. A Fediverse post is public, permanent, indexed and searchable. Silently promoting one to the other is the single worst thing an integration could do, and a channel-level toggle makes it inevitable the first time someone forgets which room they are in.

An explicit per-message action, with a visibly different affordance, is the only safe shape. It should look like publishing, not like sending.

Flow 2: reading inward

  a dedicated tab or section, NEVER mixed into mesh conversations

  ActivityPub:  poll the user's home timeline via their server's API
  AT Protocol:  fetch the timeline from an AppView

  Needs INTERNET. Degrades to empty when offline, which is fine
  because it is an extra, and disastrous if it were mixed into a
  mesh room, where "empty" would read as "the mesh is broken".

Flow 3: contact discovery

The genuinely useful one, and the one with the sharpest privacy edge. "Which of the people I follow on Bluesky also use Airhop?"

ApproachHowLeak
Publish a pointerPut your Airhop Nostr pubkey in your Bluesky profile or Mastodon bio. Others read it.Permanently and publicly links your social identity to your mesh identity. Irreversible: the link is archived, indexed and mirrored the moment it is published.
Cross-reference DIDsDerive a DID from the Airhop identity and look for it in follow graphs.Same link, same permanence.
Private set intersectionCryptographic protocol to find the overlap without revealing either list.Much better, and much harder. Needs a party to run it or a multi-round protocol between clients.

The trap in this feature

The whole point of an Airhop identity is that it is not linked to a real-world identity. Publishing your mesh key on a social profile deletes that property permanently, for everyone who has ever seen the profile, including archives. It also creates a selector: an adversary who knows your Bluesky account now knows your Nostr pubkey, and can watch for it on relays and in geohash cells.

If it ships at all, it needs a confirmation that says exactly that, in those words, and the default must be off.

The privacy analysis

ConcernSeverityDetail
Identity linkage high Connecting an Airhop identity to a public social account is permanent and public. Nothing walks it back.
Server holds a key high ActivityPub instances hold the key that signs your deliveries and can post as you. AT Protocol is better here: records are signed by your own key.
Content permanence medium A mesh message is ephemeral, unindexed and local. A federated post is archived, indexed, searchable, and mirrored to servers you have never heard of. Deletion is a polite request.
Metadata to a server medium Your IP, your posting times, your follow graph. Tor helps for the transport and not for the account.
Accidental promotion high Posting to a mesh room and to the public internet must never be the same gesture.
Attack surface medium New HTTP clients, new parsers, JSON-LD for ActivityPub (a notoriously complex format), OAuth flows. All new code reachable from remote input.
Dependency low Only if it is optional and off by default, which the roadmap specifies.
Moderation model clash low Airhop cannot moderate at the protocol level and says so. A Fediverse instance can and does. A user bridging content may be surprised that their instance removes it.

Does it break the app

4. No central servercomplicated The project still operates none. The user's instance or PDS is a server they chose, like a Nostr relay or a mint.

"Not a centralized social network"holds No profiles are hosted, no search index is operated.

So the architectural answer is: it does not break the app, provided it is genuinely a plugin. The positioning answer is that principle 3 would need to be restated as "the app never requires an account" rather than "no accounts, ever".

The plugin architecture

The roadmap specifies the constraints, and they are the right ones.

ConstraintWhy it is the right rule
A generic Plugin interface in src/core/, with typed subtypes per categoryKeeps integration code out of the mesh path entirely.
Per-plugin opt-in with explicit permission promptsNothing happens because a user updated the app.
Strict data boundary: plugins see only what the user marks shareable, never mesh trafficThis is the load-bearing rule. A plugin that could read the mesh would be a exfiltration path with a friendly name.
Capability model: no key access or network call on the user's behalf without a per-action confirmationPrevents the classic failure where an integration quietly posts on your behalf.
Separately auditableA reviewer can read the plugin without reading the mesh, and vice versa.
Users who enable none are unaffectedThe mesh protocol and wire format are unchanged.

The same v1.2.0 milestone also lists a UPI payment plugin for India, and it is a useful contrast because the roadmap is unusually blunt about it: online only, opt in, disabled by default, with a disclosure on enable that transactions are linked to a verified identity and visible to NPCI. It is included as a convenience, explicitly not as a private rail, and Cashu remains the offline payment system. That is exactly the tone a Fediverse bridge should adopt.

My verdict

Arguments for

  • Distribution. The honest weakness of a phone mesh is that nobody else has the app. A bridge is a discovery channel into communities that already value decentralisation.
  • Ideological fit. Both are open, both are federated, both are the audience most likely to install this.
  • AT Protocol maps cleanly. Records signed by your own key and a portable DID are much closer to Airhop's model than anything else on offer.
  • Genuinely useful in a disaster. "Post this to the internet from a phone that has signal, on behalf of a crowd that does not" is a real scenario, and the gateway already proves the shape.
  • It costs nothing to users who leave it off.

Arguments against

  • Airhop already has an internet layer, and it is better: Nostr needs no account, no server, no key held by anyone else. This adds a second one that is strictly weaker on every privacy axis.
  • Identity linkage is irreversible and is the exact opposite of what the product exists for.
  • Accidental promotion from a local room to the public internet is the worst failure mode the app could have, and no UI is perfect.
  • It is a large amount of new remote-facing code: HTTP clients, JSON-LD, OAuth, XRPC. In an app whose selling point is a small, auditable protocol core.
  • The opportunity cost is high. Fanout subsetting, prekey sync, Arti on Android, Tor bridges and the LAN transport are all higher value for the mission.

What I would actually do

  1. Do not build it before v1.9.0. The items in chapter 49 serve the mission directly; this serves growth.
  2. If it is built, do AT Protocol first. Records are signed by the user's own key, the DID is portable, and the identity mapping is real rather than aspirational. ActivityPub's server-holds-your-key model is the weaker fit.
  3. Outbound publishing only, at first. Per message, explicit, with an affordance that looks like publishing rather than sending. No inbound timeline, which is the largest surface for the least benefit.
  4. Never ship automatic contact discovery. If it ships, it must require an explicit confirmation that states plainly that the link between the two identities is public and permanent.
  5. Restate principle 3 honestly as "the app never requires an account" rather than quietly weakening "no accounts, ever".
  6. Treat it as a separate package. The plugin should be independently auditable and, ideally, independently installable, so a user who wants none of it ships none of it.

Drills

  • What is the Fediverse, in three sentences? A network of independent servers speaking ActivityPub, a W3C standard where a user is an Actor with an inbox and an outbox, and posts are Activities wrapping Objects. Publishing means your home server POSTs the activity to every follower's inbox, signed with a key the server holds. Identity is @user@server, so it is bound to that server, and if the server closes the identity goes with it.
  • How does AT Protocol differ, and why does that matter to Airhop? It is aggregation rather than federation: you write signed records into your own personal data server, a relay crawls every PDS into one firehose, and AppViews build timelines from it. Two things matter here. Records are signed by your key rather than a server's, and identity is a portable DID that survives moving servers. That is much closer to Airhop's model than ActivityPub's, which is why it is the better first integration despite Bluesky's practical centralisation around one relay and one AppView.
  • Does bridging require account creation, and does that break the app? Yes it requires an account: ActivityPub needs a home server that hosts your actor and holds a key, and AT Protocol needs a PDS even if you self-host it. It does not break the architecture, because a plugin that never touches mesh traffic or private keys leaves the offline-first and wire-compatibility principles intact. It does break the wording of principle 3, "no accounts, ever", which would have to be restated as "the app never requires an account". That is a positioning change, and it should be made openly rather than quietly.
  • What is the single most dangerous part of this feature? Contact discovery by publishing your Airhop key on a social profile. It permanently and publicly links a mesh identity, whose entire value is being unlinked from a real-world identity, to an indexed and archived social account. It is irreversible, it creates a selector an adversary can watch for on relays and in geohash cells, and it is exactly the property the app exists to protect. Second most dangerous is accidental promotion: making "post to this room" and "publish to the internet" the same gesture.
  • Airhop already has Nostr. Why add another internet layer at all? The only honest answer is distribution, not capability. Nostr is strictly better on every privacy axis: no account, no home server, no key held by anyone else, and gift wrap that hides sender and recipient from relays. What a Fediverse bridge buys is reach into communities that already value decentralisation and are the most likely people to install a mesh messenger. That is a real benefit, and it is a growth argument rather than a mission argument, which is why I would sequence it behind the items that serve the mission.
