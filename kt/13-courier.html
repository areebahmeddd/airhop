In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Your friend is not here and there is no phone signal. So you write the message, lock it in a box only your friend's key can open, and hand the box to four people who are walking off in different directions. None of them can open it. None of them knows who it is for. If one of them bumps into your friend, they hand it over. If they meet someone else carrying boxes, they share some of theirs. After a day, everyone throws away any box they are still carrying.

Delay-tolerant networking

The internet assumes an end-to-end path exists at the moment you send. DTN is the field that drops that assumption. It grew out of interplanetary networking, where the round trip to Mars is between 6 and 44 minutes and the link is blocked whenever a planet is in the way, and it turns out to describe a protest with no signal just as well.

ExampleTCPBundle Protocol (RFC 9171), Airhop's courier

The academic name for Airhop's setting is a pocket switched network: nodes are carried by people, movement is social rather than random, and encounters are the resource. The two things that decide performance are how often nodes meet, and how many copies of a message you are willing to make.

The envelope

  COURIER_ENV (0x04) payload, TLV with u16 BE lengths

    0x01  recipientTag   16 bytes   HMAC over the recipient's public key + day
    0x02  expiry          8 bytes   Unix ms, u64 BE
    0x03  ciphertext     variable   Noise X sealed, max 16 KiB
    0x04  copies          1 byte    spray budget. OMITTED when copies == 1
    0x05  prekeyID        4 bytes   v2 only: which one-time prekey this seals to

  The packet itself:
    ttl 7, SIGNED, HAS_RECIPIENT set to the COURIER, not the recipient

That last line is the one people get wrong. The packet's recipientID names the courier being asked to carry it, not the person the message is for. The person it is for is identified only by the opaque 16 byte tag inside.

Directed rather than broadcast, for three reasons:

  • bitchat refuses any envelope not addressed to it.
  • The deposit quota is per depositor, so a broadcast would charge one deposit against every peer in range.
  • A broadcast tells everyone in earshot that this device is couriering, which is metadata worth not emitting.

It is still flood-originated as well as written down the direct link, because a courier may be several hops away. The recipient field stops relays from depositing it. But only a write accepted onto that exact peer's own link counts as a handover: a speculative flood toward a peer several hops away resolves false, which is not a failure but the signal to keep carrying, since a multi-hop send has nothing to acknowledge it.

The recipient tag, and its flaw

  epochDay = floor(unixSeconds / 86400)          as u32 BE

  recipientTag = HMAC-SHA256(
                    key = recipient's PUBLIC Noise static key,
                    msg = "bitchat-courier-tag-v1" || epochDay_BE4
                 )[0:16]

The intent is that a carrier can match a delivery without learning who the envelope is for. When a peer announces, you compute their tag for today, yesterday and tomorrow, and check whether anything in your bag matches.

Three days rather than two, and this matters: the tag rotates on a UTC epoch day while an envelope lives 24 hours, so anything carried across midnight bears yesterday's tag, and a sender whose clock runs ahead seals with tomorrow's. Checking only backwards silently loses mail once a day. bitchat's candidateTags spans the same three days for the same reason.

The tag is NOT unlinkable, and this must be said plainly

The HMAC key is the recipient's public Noise static key, which every announce broadcasts in the clear. So anyone who has heard one announce can compute that peer's tags for any day, past or future, and follow their mail across days.

This is inherited from bitchat rather than chosen. bitchat documents the same flaw in its own implementation and notes that its whitepaper's claim that couriers cannot link mail across days does not hold. Airhop keeps the derivation byte for byte because changing it unilaterally breaks courier interop with bitchat for no gain: every carrier on both sides would have to change together.

Fixing it needs a v2 tag keyed on a shared secret rather than a public key, agreed across implementations. Until then, do not describe courier mail as unlinkable. What it does provide is confidentiality (the carrier cannot read the content) and non-attribution of the sender to the carrier.

Spray and wait

The classic DTN question is how many copies to make. Two extremes bound it:

StrategyCopiesDeliveryCost
Direct delivery1Only if you personally meet the recipientMinimal
Epidemic routingUnboundedOptimal, provably fastestEvery node ends up holding every message. Storage and airtime explode.
Spray and waitBounded, LClose to epidemic for small LBounded by construction

Spray and wait was published by Spyropoulos, Psounis and Raghavendra in 2005 and is the standard bounded-copy scheme. It has two phases: spray L copies into the crowd, then each holder waits for direct contact with the destination. Binary spray, the variant used here and proven optimal in the family, hands over half the remaining budget on each courier-to-courier encounter. 

  SENDER seals ONCE and hands the SAME BYTES to up to 4 couriers,
  each stamped with the FULL budget of 4.

     A(4)          A(4)          A(4)          A(4)
      │             │             │             │
      ▼             ▼             ▼             ▼
     B(4)          C(4)          D(4)          E(4)

  B meets F, another eligible carrier. Binary spray:

     B(4)  ──▶  gives F floor(4/2) = 2,  keeps 4-2 = 2
     B(2)       F(2)

  F meets G:

     F(2)  ──▶  gives G floor(2/2) = 1,  keeps 1
     F(1)       G(1)

  A copy at 1 cannot spray. It is the end of its branch, and can only
  be delivered directly to the recipient.

Why the full budget goes to every courier

Splitting the budget across couriers looks conservative and is not: three couriers would get one copy each, a budget below two cannot be sprayed at all, and the busier the room the less the mail travels. The full budget to each is safe only because CourierStore.deposit collapses identical ciphertext and raises a budget only before the first spray, so copies merge downward wherever carriers meet rather than multiplying. The two rules belong together; neither is correct alone.

Three exclusions in the spray pass, each load bearing

ExclusionWithout it
copies < 2Nothing to halve. A carry-only copy is the end of its branch.
sprayedTo set, once per peerAnnounces arrive continuously, so a budget would decay 4 to 2 to 1 against one neighbour without ever reaching a new carrier.
The depositorThey handed us this envelope, so they demonstrably hold it. Spraying back spends half a budget on a hop that delivers nothing, and leaves a sender carrying their own outgoing mail as third-party mail.

bitchat also excludes envelopes addressed to the peer being sprayed. Here that is structural instead: the caller runs the handover pass first, so anything for them has already been offered as a delivery rather than as a spray.

Handover versus spray

Two distinct operations, in this order, never merged:

  1. Handover. Mail addressed to this peer. They are the destination, so the copy carries no spray budget (copies: 1) and the envelope is retired once it lands. Budget-independent: an envelope down to its last copy is exactly the one a carrier standing next to the recipient must still hand over.
  2. Spray. Mail for somebody else, offered to them as another carrier. Half the remaining budget, once per peer.

Both are non-destructive until the transport confirms. The store offers copies, the caller writes them, and only a write accepted onto that peer's own link calls commitHandover or commitSpray. Retiring before then loses mail to a refused write, and refusals are ordinary here: this runs from onAnnounce, when the link is busiest with the announce, the prekey bundle and a gossip round, and a full GATT queue answers with a refusal.

commitSpray re-checks every precondition rather than trusting the offer, since the write is async and a concurrent spray or expiry sweep may have moved the entry. copies > given is what stops a stale commit driving the budget to zero.

Quotas and the pool

Your phone is being asked to spend storage and battery on somebody else's mail. The quotas decide how much of that you agree to.

LimitValueWhat it prevents
Pool size40 envelopesUnbounded storage growth.
Verified-tier sub-cap20 envelopesEnough distinct verified strangers filling the pool between them and leaving no room for people you actually know.
Per-depositor quota, contact5One friend monopolising the bag.
Per-depositor quota, stranger2An unknown peer filling your storage.

Expiry slack1 hourClock skew between two phones, and nothing more. An expiry past TTL plus slack is refused outright rather than clamped, so a sender that stamps longer does not get longer carriage, it gets none.
Spray budget ceiling8copies is unauthenticated input deciding how often a carrier re-emits. An unclamped byte makes every carrier an amplifier for whoever claims 255.

Trust tiers

A depositor is a contact (larger quota) or verified (smaller). Verified means the announce was signature-checked and the peer ID matched its key, which is the minimum bar for being charged a quota at all. An unknown depositor gets nothing, because there is no key to charge against.

The depositor's identity is checked before the store is touched. The quota is charged to packet.senderID, which is an unauthenticated header field, so without a signature check any peer in range could put a known peer's ID on an envelope and spend that peer's allowance, or at the contact tier a friend's larger one.

Eviction

  findEvictionCandidate(incoming tier):
    1. the OLDEST VERIFIED envelope, whatever the arrival is
    2. if none: only another CONTACT envelope may take a contact's slot
    3. otherwise refuse the deposit

A verified arrival may never displace a contact's mail, because that would let anyone who has merely announced push a friend's mail out of a full pool. bitchat states the same rule.

Persistence

The bag is persisted to MMKV, because carriage spans hours and a React Native process does not: backgrounding, an OEM battery manager and a swipe away all end it, and an in-memory bag loses every envelope this device promised to carry while the depositor's outbox still believes a courier holds it.

MMKV rather than the keychain, because an envelope is somebody else's ciphertext, sealed to a key this device does not hold. It is wiped on panic all the same, since a bag of a neighbour's mail is evidence this phone stood next to whoever deposited it, and a recipient tag names who it is for to anyone who heard that person announce.

On load, every row is re-validated field by field and the pool cap is re-applied. This is read after an upgrade, a crash mid-write or a restored partition, and a malformed row must cost one envelope rather than the whole bag. A ceiling lowered in a later build must bind the mail already on disk.

One-time prekeys and forward secrecy

Sealing to a static key is the courier's main cryptographic weakness, and prekeys are the fix.

v1: sealed to the static key

Noise X to the recipient's long-lived X25519 key. No forward secrecy. If that key is ever compromised, every undelivered envelope ever sealed to it becomes readable, including ones an adversary recorded off the air months earlier.

v2: sealed to a one-time prekey

Noise X to a single-use X25519 key the recipient published in a signed bundle. Once used and consumed, the private half is deleted after a grace window, so compromising the long-lived key later reveals nothing about that envelope. 

  PREKEY LIFECYCLE

  LocalPrekeyStore (mine)                PeerPrekeyStore (theirs)
  ─────────────────────                  ────────────────────────
  generate a pool of X25519 keypairs      ingest a VERIFIED bundle
  sign a bundle over the public halves      keyed on their Noise pub key
  flood it as PREKEY_BUNDLE (0x24)          newer generatedAt replaces older
  on every new link                         cap: 200 peers, oldest evicted

  someone seals to prekey #7               assign() picks an UNUSED prekey
  I open it with priv #7                     marks it used so the next
  consume(7):                                 message picks a different one
    move to a 48 HOUR GRACE window
    (a second in-flight envelope sealed
     to the same key must still open)
    replenish the pool
    emit a FRESH bundle, so senders
    stop using the spent key

  after 48 hours: the private key is DELETED. Forward secrecy achieved.

The bundle is signed over the Noise static public key plus the prekeys plus a generatedAt counter, so a receiver can confirm it belongs to the peer it claims and can order two bundles. The prekey store is capped at 200 peers, matching the peer registry, because bundles arrive by gossip and are therefore attacker-supplied volume.

The gap

Prekey bundles are flooded on link-up but not reconciled through gossip sync (bit 9 is unimplemented, chapter 12). So a device that arrives after a bundle has already flooded cannot pull one from a peer that still holds it, and a courier message sealed to that owner falls back to their long-lived static key. The message is still delivered and still encrypted. What is lost is forward secrecy for that envelope, silently, with nothing in the UI to say so.

What is inside, and the 255 byte cap

The sealed plaintext is a typed Noise payload, not raw text. This is a compatibility requirement rather than a preference: bitchat refuses any courier envelope whose plaintext is not a private message, so a raw-text envelope is carried by the mesh and then dropped by its recipient. 

  plaintext = 0x01 || PrivateMessagePacket

  PrivateMessagePacket = 0x00 || len(messageID) || messageID
                         0x01 || len(content)   || content

  Both TLV lengths are ONE BYTE.
  => messageID and content are each capped at 255 UTF-8 BYTES,
     on all three implementations, and all three return nothing
     rather than truncating.

A message too long to encode has no courier representation anywhere and stays in the sender's outbox instead. Refusing is honest; truncating or sealing something no recipient can read is not.

The message ID is what makes the rest work. Spray and wait deliberately puts several copies on the mesh, each resealed by its carrier with a fresh timestamp, so no envelope-derived identity can collapse them. The recipient dedupes on the sender's ID, and can acknowledge the message because it has one to name. Building the id from the relaying carrier's clock instead gives four carriers four identical bubbles for one message, and never collapses a courier copy against a direct copy.

An earlier version of Airhop sealed bare UTF-8. Every envelope Airhop sent was dropped by every bitchat recipient, and every bitchat envelope Airhop received rendered a binary TLV as the message body, while the store's own header claimed compatibility. These bytes are now pinned by courier-plaintext.test.ts, and every constant in this chapter is pinned in machine-readable form in docs/spec/courier-test-vectors.json, which courier-vectors.test.ts reads its expected values out of, so drift between the spec and the code fails CI rather than shipping.

The relay drop

When the internet is available, the same sealed envelope is also parked on Nostr relays as a kind 1401 event, tagged with the same recipient tag under #x, with a NIP-40 expiry.

Mesh copyRelay drop
Carried byUp to 4 peers, sprayed onwardRelays
Spray budget41, always
Delivery conditionA carrier physically meets the recipientThe recipient subscribes to their own tag
Reported as"Carried by a friend"Queued. Nobody carried it and it never touched the mesh.

The relay copy carries a budget of 1 because it is carry-only: it goes to the recipient, not to another carrier, so it must never arrive with a spray budget and start a second branch. The recipient collapses both copies on the sender's message ID.

The subscription is rebuilt when the UTC day changes, because the tag set it was built for has rotated.

Dormant, woken, delivered

A carried envelope does nothing at all until something wakes it. There is exactly one trigger, and it is worth stating plainly because it explains every question about when a courier moves.

The one trigger

An ANNOUNCE from any peer, over any transport. onAnnounce calls flushOutbox(peerID) for our own mail, then sprayCourierTo(peerID) for other people's. There is no courier timer, no polling loop, and no background scan of the bag. A phone carrying forty envelopes and meeting nobody burns no cycles on them. 

  DORMANT                                    persisted in MMKV
  ───────                                    survives app kill
  nothing runs. no timer, no poll.           survives reboot
        │
        │  someone announces. anyone, over anything.
        ▼
  onAnnounce(peerID)
        │
        ├─ registry.get(peerID).noisePubKey ?      no  -> stop
        │     we cannot compute their tag without it
        │
        ├─ compute THREE candidate tags
        │     computeRecipientTag(peerPub, now)
        │     computeRecipientTag(peerPub, now - 24h)
        │     computeRecipientTag(peerPub, now + 24h)
        │
        ├─ 1. HANDOVER   offerHandover(tags)
        │      envelopes whose tag matches: this peer is the
        │      DESTINATION. no spray budget spent, envelope
        │      retired once it lands. budget-independent, because
        │      an envelope on its last copy is exactly the one a
        │      carrier standing next to the recipient must hand over.
        │
        └─ 2. SPRAY      offerSpray(peerPub)
               everything else, offered to them as another CARRIER.
               half the remaining budget, once per peer.

  Handover first, always. That is also why the spray pass needs no
  "addressed to this peer" exclusion of its own: by then there are
  none left.

How it knows the destination without knowing the destination

The carrier never learns who an envelope is for. It holds a recipient tag, and it can only ever ask one question: does this tag match the tag I would compute for the peer standing in front of me. It computes that from the peer's public Noise key, which it already has from their announce.

Three days of tags, not one

The tag is stamped by the sender at seal time and rotates on the UTC epoch day, while an envelope lives 24 hours. So an envelope carried across midnight bears yesterday's tag, and a sender whose clock runs ahead seals with tomorrow's. Checking only today missed most of what a carrier actually held, including a device carrying its own mail around instead of opening it.

Both the receive gate and the handover pass check all three, which matches bitchat's candidateTags spanning the same window.

How it picks a transport: it does not

This is the part that surprises people, and it is the whole payoff of the link registry in chapter 14. 

  sendCourierPayloadTo(payload, peerID)

     build a COURIER_ENV packet, ttl 7, SIGNED,
     recipientID = peerID          directed, never broadcast
        │
        ├─ floodRouter.originate(packet)
        │     so a courier several hops away can still be reached
        │
        ├─ const link = this.links.linkFor(peerID)
        │      ▲
        │      └── THE ENTIRE TRANSPORT DECISION.
        │          returns the best link this peer has, in
        │          SEND_PREFERENCE order: wifi, lan, ble.
        │          the courier code does not branch on kind,
        │          does not know which one it got, and has no
        │          transport-specific path anywhere in it.
        │
        ├─ link  -> links.send(link.id, bytes)   ->  TRUE if accepted
        └─ none  -> links.broadcast(bytes)       ->  always FALSE

  TRUE means the bytes went out over a link held to THAT EXACT PEER
  and the transport accepted them. Only then is the envelope retired,
  or the spray budget spent.

  A speculative flood toward a peer several hops away resolves FALSE.
  That is not a failure: it is the signal to keep carrying, because a
  multi-hop send has nothing to acknowledge it. bitchat draws the same
  line between handoverEnvelopes and envelopesForRemoteHandover.

The consequence worth stating outright

An envelope picked up over LAN on a hotspot, carried in a pocket through a reboot, and delivered over Bluetooth in a different building the next morning is an ordinary path, not a special case. Nothing in the courier knows or cares which transport an envelope arrived on or leaves on. Chapter 29 traces exactly that scenario.

Nostr is the one exception, and it is a different mechanism rather than a fourth link: a copy is also parked on relays as a kind 1401 drop, so delivery stops requiring a carrier to physically meet the recipient. That copy carries copies: 1, because a parked drop goes to the recipient rather than to another carrier and must never arrive with a spray budget and start a second branch.

Why a carrier only offers to peers it holds a link to

courierCandidates() is links.directPeers() filtered to those with a known Noise key. Not peers heard through the mesh, not contacts, not everyone in the registry. Three reasons, all of them costs somebody else pays:

  • bitchat refuses any envelope not addressed to it, so a broadcast is wasted air on every interoperating client.
  • The deposit quota is per depositor, so a broadcast charges one deposit against every peer in range at once.
  • A broadcast reveals to everyone in earshot that this device is couriering, which is metadata the directed form does not leak.

Alternatives from the DTN literature

SchemeHow it decidesVerdict for Airhop
Epidemic routing (Vahdat and Becker, 2000) Copy to every node you meet. Provably the fastest possible delivery. Unbounded storage and airtime. Every phone ends up holding every message in the crowd. Rejected everywhere for the same reason.
Spray and wait (2005) Bounded L copies, binary halving on encounter. What Airhop uses. Bounded by construction, close to epidemic performance at small L, and needs no knowledge of the network.
PRoPHET Track how often you meet each node and forward to peers with a higher delivery predictability for the destination. Better delivery in stable social networks. Requires maintaining an encounter history per peer, which is a privacy disaster: it is a record of everyone you have met and how often. bitchat lists encounter-history-informed routing as future work; Airhop should not take it without solving that.
MaxProp Priority ordering by path likelihood and hop count. Same objection, plus complexity that is hard to make interoperable across implementations.
Bundle Protocol (RFC 9171) The IETF standard: custody transfer, endpoint IDs, convergence layers. Designed for named endpoints and administrative routing. Airhop's whole point is that there are no names to route on, only opaque tags.
Data mules / sneakernet A dedicated carrier that physically moves between partitions. Not a protocol change, an operational one. A volunteer walking between two ends of a march is a data mule, and it works with no code at all.

If you removed the courier entirely

DMs to anyone not currently reachable would sit in the sender's outbox until either the recipient came back into radio range or the internet returned. In a total blackout with a moving crowd, that is a large fraction of all messages. The courier is what makes "message someone across a march with no signal" work when they are not within seven hops right now. It is also the single feature furthest from anything a normal messenger does, and the hardest for a user to reason about, which is why the UI says "carried by a friend" rather than showing a tick.

Drills

  • Is courier mail unlinkable? No, and it is important to say so. The tag is an HMAC keyed on the recipient's public Noise key, which every announce broadcasts. Anyone who has heard one announce can compute that peer's tags for any day and follow their mail across days. What the courier does provide is confidentiality (the carrier cannot read the content), sender non-attribution to the carrier, and no visible link between sender and recipient inside the envelope. Fixing linkability needs a v2 tag keyed on a shared secret, agreed across implementations.
  • Why does the sender seal once rather than per courier? So that identical ciphertext identifies the same envelope. That is what lets deposit collapse duplicates when two carriers meet, which is what keeps spray and wait a splitting scheme rather than a minting one. If each courier got its own encryption, every copy would be a distinct entry with its own budget and its own sprayed-to set, so one message could fill the bag and the mesh-wide copy count would grow on every encounter instead of shrinking.
  • Why do handover and spray commit only after the write is accepted? Because this runs from onAnnounce, when the link is busiest with the announce, the prekey bundle and a gossip round, and a full GATT queue answers with a refusal. Retiring an envelope on intent rather than acceptance loses mail to an ordinary transient refusal. And a speculative flood at a peer several hops away resolves false, which is not a failure but the signal to keep carrying, since a multi-hop send has nothing to acknowledge it.
  • Why three days of tags rather than one? The tag rotates on a UTC epoch day and an envelope lives 24 hours, so anything carried across midnight bears yesterday's tag. And a sender whose clock runs ahead across the boundary seals with tomorrow's, which is why the forward day is checked too. Checking only today silently loses mail once a day, and checking only backwards silently loses the clock-ahead case. bitchat spans the same three days.
  • A device holds 40 envelopes. A contact deposits one more. What happens? The pool is full, so eviction runs. The oldest verified-tier envelope goes first, whatever the arrival is. If the pool is all contact-tier mail, then because the incoming envelope is also contact-tier it may displace the oldest one. Had the incoming envelope been from a stranger, it would have been refused instead, because letting a verified arrival displace a contact's mail would let anyone who has merely announced push a friend's mail out.
