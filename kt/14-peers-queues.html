Peers, sessions and queues

Between the wire format and the screens sits a layer of bookkeeping: who is out there, what we know about them, whether we can reach them, and what we still owe them. Get any of it wrong and messages vanish while the interface says everything is fine.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Your phone keeps two lists. One is an address book: people it has met, and the fingerprint that proves each one is really them. That list never forgets. The other is a list of who it can hear right now, and that one is constantly changing as people walk around. It also keeps a small pile of letters it has not managed to deliver yet, and every time somebody comes back into earshot it checks the pile for anything addressed to them.

Identity is not reachability

The single most important distinction in this chapter

"Do I know who this is?" and "Can I reach them right now?" are different questions with different lifetimes, and conflating them was a real bug more than once.

Two bugs from getting this wrong

Genuine departures were refused. A LEAVE arrives precisely when a peer has stopped announcing, so resolving its signing key through the reachability window rejected the real ones. Fixed by pinnedSigningKey(), which ignores the TTL entirely.

Inbound DMs were dropped. Decryption resolved the Noise session through get(), so a message from a direct peer whose announce had aged out was discarded, while the sender's sendDm reported "sent" and the outbox queued no retry. Fixed by sessionFor(): the packet having arrived is the presence that the window only estimates.

The peer registry

Everything in this chapter is owned by one object. What that object is, how it is built and torn down, and how a packet moves through it are 15.

PeerRegistry in core/router/message-router.ts is the protocol layer's model of everyone it knows about.

    peerID:      string          16 hex chars
    noisePubKey: Uint8Array      32 bytes X25519, for sealing and courier tags
    signingPubKey: Uint8Array    32 bytes Ed25519, for verifying their packets
    nickname:    string          self-asserted, decoration only

    authenticatedCapabilities?: number   proven inside a Noise session (0x21)

    bridgeGeohash?: string       rendezvous cell a bridge peer advertises

The three write paths, in order of authority

PathTrustMay create a pinMay replace a pin
update() from an ANNOUNCESelf-signed, so self-consistent onlyYes, if none existsNever

An announce that conflicts with a pinned key is rejected entirely, not merged. The nickname and capability bits in a conflicting announce come from the same unverified source, so accepting any of it would still let an attacker rewrite how that peer is presented. And update() can never lower authenticatedCapabilities or clear signingKeyAuthenticated, which is what stops a later announce quietly reducing a proof back to a claim.

The cap, and why direct peers survive it

  MAX_TRACKED_PEERS = 200

  enforceCap():
     take every entry that is NOT direct
     sort oldest first
     delete until back under the cap

  A DIRECT PEER IS NEVER EVICTED.

The reasoning is worth stating precisely. Registry entries come from announces, which are unauthenticated input: anyone in radio range can mint unlimited internally consistent identities with real key pairs, correctly derived peer IDs and valid self-signatures. Nothing about any individual one is detectably wrong, which is what makes a Sybil flood a resource problem rather than a signature problem.

Reads were always TTL-bounded, so a flood never made a fake peer look reachable. But the underlying map grew without limit and was never swept, so the memory was held for the life of the process and every reachablePeers() scan walked all of it. The cap fixes that, and protecting direct peers is what makes the cap meaningful: a direct peer holds a physical link, which no amount of announcing can fake, so they are simultaneously the entries most worth keeping and the ones an attacker most wants pushed out.

The link registry

A link is a live radio connection with exactly one peer on the far end. That last part is the whole security property. 

  ONE REGISTRY, EVERY TRANSPORT      core/mesh/links/link-registry.ts

  kindByLink     linkID -> ble | wifi | lan       what opened it
  peerByLink     linkID -> peerID                 who is on it
  linksByPeer    peerID -> [linkID, ...]          how to reach them

  peerByLink is trustworthy BY CONSTRUCTION. A senderID header is
  plaintext and forgeable; the far end of a socket we hold is not.

  linksByPeer is a LIST, not one slot per transport. Bluetooth is
  dual-role, so two phones that meet each dial the other and hold
  TWO BLE links to the same peer. One slot cannot represent that,
  and closing either link would unbind the other.

  TRANSPORT_KINDS   ble, wifi, lan     what order do we LIST things
  SEND_PREFERENCE   wifi, lan, ble     which link do we USE

  Two different questions, so two different orders, and both are
  load-bearing. See the note below each.

The rule the link registry is built around

One question gets one answer covering every transport, never an answer per transport for the caller to combine. Before it, the mesh service held four maps, two per transport, and every caller that wanted "can I reach this peer" had to ask twice and merge. Adding LAN would have made it six maps and three questions. Adding a transport is now a member of TRANSPORT_KINDS and a writer for it.

It also decides nothing. Which packet goes where, what makes a peer count as direct, and what a refused write means all stay in mesh-service.ts. The writers are injected and nothing in the file imports native code, so it runs in CI without a radio.

degree() counts Bluetooth PEERS, and both halves were bugs

The flood router scales relay jitter and the time-critical TTL cap by how crowded the mesh looks. That count used to be links, across every transport, and both parts were wrong.

Peers, not links, because Bluetooth is dual-role: two phones that meet each dial the other, so one neighbour contributed two links and every room read as twice as crowded as it was. The dense threshold was crossed at three real peers where bitchat crosses at six, clamping voice and fragment range to five hops earlier than it should.

Bluetooth only, because the delay exists for radio contention: every phone in earshot hears a packet at the same instant, and rebroadcasting together drowns the room out. A socket on LAN or WiFi Aware does not compete for that airtime, so counting it slows the radio down for no reason.

Both halves now match bitchat's peerRegistry.connectedCount. The fix is invisible to the simulator, which models one link per pair; on hardware the degree roughly halves.

Why the two orders are both load-bearing

SEND_PREFERENCE is wifi, lan, ble, fastest first. WiFi Aware leads because it is a direct radio link with no access point in the path; LAN goes phone to router to phone, still far past Bluetooth's roughly 18 KiB/s; Bluetooth is the floor and the fallback.

TRANSPORT_KINDS is ble, wifi, lan, and it is the enumeration order, which is observable. The announce carries only the first ten directPeers() as TLV 0x04, and that list is the mesh graph other clients draw. A phone on a busy network holds more LAN peers than Bluetooth ones, so LAN goes last deliberately: letting it crowd out the Bluetooth neighbours would hand bitchat a graph full of edges it cannot use.

The Sybil vector that defeated every other cap

A peer counted as directly connected whenever packet.ttl === 7, on the reasoning that relays decrement, so a full TTL means it came straight from its author. True, and TTL is a plaintext header field an attacker sets freely.

So one hostile radio, over one real link, could announce hundreds of invented identities that all looked directly connected. Each overwrote the link's peer binding (today peerByLink in the link registry), breaking RSSI attribution and disconnect handling for the genuine peer on it. And because direct peers are protected from eviction, every one of them was immune to trimming. Five hundred invented peers survived a flood the caps existed to bound.

The fix pairs the TTL heuristic with a physical fact: a link binds to the first peer that announces directly on it, and a later claim from a different peer ID on that same link is downgraded to relayed rather than dropped. Downgrading is the gentler equivalent of bitchat's outright directSenderMismatch rejection: the announce is still useful topology, it simply does not earn direct standing. Scenario C04.

EventEffect
linkConnectedA link exists but nobody is bound to it yet. The peer is unknown until an announce arrives.

Relayed announceUpdate the entry, but pass isDirect: undefined so an existing direct standing is preserved rather than demoted. The flood router delivers whichever copy arrives first, so a relayed copy easily precedes the direct one.
linkDisconnectedmarkIndirect(). The peer may still be reachable through the mesh until its announce expires.

Two caps bound the link layer. Six concurrent central links on both platforms, because Android controllers support roughly seven GATT client connections and refuse the rest with an opaque status 133. And a 15 second reaper that reclaims a duplicate link, which happens because iOS rotates its Bluetooth address and the same device reappears as a new advertiser.

The one-message asymmetry

The initiator completes on msg2, one message before the responder completes on msg3. Anything sent in that window is decrypted by nobody: the initiator has a session and the responder does not yet. Airhop lost first-contact DMs and group invites to exactly this. The fix is that tryInitDR and the queued-invite flush run after msg3 goes out rather than at split.

A second asymmetry follows from the ratchet itself: a receiver-initialised state has no sending chain until it receives something, so the side that answered the handshake cannot speak first. That is the algorithm working correctly, and it is why there is an explicit canEncrypt() predicate and a fallback to plain Noise transport, rather than an exception on the send path.

Sessions are deliberately not persisted. The cost is a re-handshake after every restart. The benefit is that a seized device holds no live session keys on disk, and that a peer which restarted and discarded its own session cannot leave the other side sealing messages nobody can open. That second case is exactly why every mesh send is enqueued in the outbox regardless of apparent success.

The outbox

The queue behind "queued for delivery". Without it, the promise that a message will arrive when the recipient returns is a lie and every out-of-range DM is lost.

The bug that made "always enqueue" the rule

A DM over an established session with a direct link returned "sent" and was never queued. If the peer had restarted and discarded its session, they received nothing while the sender saw a tick. Now every mesh send is enqueued, matching bitchat's rule that nothing counts as delivered without an authenticated acknowledgement. The cost is a queue entry that lives for a few seconds, because a receipt arrives well inside the flush interval.

One further re-keying detail: when an announce reveals that a Nostr pubkey belongs to a known peer ID, anything queued against the nostr_<pubkey> identifier is resolved and re-enqueued under the peer ID, so it now takes the cheaper offline-capable mesh route. The chat store merges the two threads at the same moment, so the user sees one conversation instead of the same person twice.

Outbox against courier, the distinction that matters

The outbox is ours: our messages, in plaintext, on our device, retried by us when a route appears. Nothing about it goes on the wire, which is why its TTL can diverge from bitchat's freely.

The courier bag is theirs: somebody else's ciphertext, sealed to a key this device does not hold, carried on their behalf. Its every constant is fixed by the wire protocol, because a carrier applies its own limits to envelopes it did not write. Anything Airhop exceeds is dropped by the other side, and anything it fails to enforce is a slot someone else can take.

Two rules the radar enforces that look like UI details and are not

RSSI is not liveness. updateRssi is a no-op for an unknown peer, and a signal reading never refreshes lastSeenMs. A locally polled RSSI says a radio is nearby, not that a peer is present and running the app. Only a packet that arrived proves that, which is why noteHeard() exists separately and is called only when a packet successfully decrypts: that cannot be replayed or forged, so it is stronger evidence than the announce it stands in for.

Direct standing follows the link, not the announce. setDirect is driven by the link lifecycle. A link is physical: it cannot be claimed, only held.

Drills

  • Why are there two different accessors for a peer's signing key? Because identity and reachability are different questions with different lifetimes. get() applies a 45 to 60 second TTL and answers "can I route to them". pinnedSigningKey() ignores the TTL and answers "do I know who they are". A LEAVE arrives precisely when a peer has stopped announcing, so verifying it through the reachability window would refuse the genuine ones. The same split governs inbound decryption: sessionFor() ignores the TTL because the packet having arrived is the presence the window only estimates.
  • Why is a direct peer never evicted, and how is "direct" established? Because a direct peer holds an actual GATT link, which is physical and cannot be claimed by announcing, so it is the one entry worth protecting when the list is trimmed. It is established by the link lifecycle rather than by packet contents: a link binds to the first peer that announces directly on it, and a later claim from a different peer ID on that link is downgraded to relayed. Inferring it from ttl === 7 alone was a real Sybil vector, because TTL is a plaintext field an attacker sets freely, and five hundred invented peers survived a flood the caps existed to bound.
  • Why does an ordinary link drop keep the Noise session, but a verified LEAVE retire it? A link drop is meaningless: radios drop links constantly, and resuming a session is far cheaper than a fresh three-message handshake. A verified LEAVE is a deliberate shutdown, so the peer has torn their own session down; keeping ours would mean sealing the next DM under a session that no longer exists on the far side, where it is silently discarded while being reported as sent. Only an explicit, signature-verified leave does this.
  • Why is every mesh send enqueued even when it appears to succeed? Because "the write left the radio" is not delivery. A DM over an established session with a direct link used to return sent and never queue, so a peer that had restarted and discarded its session received nothing while the sender saw a tick. Nothing counts as delivered without an authenticated acknowledgement, which is bitchat's rule too. All three receipt paths clear the entry, and a receipt arrives well inside the flush interval, so the cost is a queue entry lasting a few seconds.
  • Distinguish the outbox from the courier bag. The outbox holds our own messages, in plaintext, on our own device, retried by us when a route appears; nothing about it goes on the wire, which is why its TTL can be seven days where bitchat uses one. The courier bag holds other people's ciphertext, sealed to keys this device does not hold, carried on their behalf; every one of its constants is fixed by the wire protocol, because a carrier applies its own limits to envelopes it did not write, so anything we exceed is dropped by the other side and anything we fail to enforce is a slot someone else can take.
  • Why is the outbox cap per recipient rather than global? Because a global cap evicts the oldest entry whatever it is, so one chatty unreachable peer silently deletes queued mail for a different conversation, and the sender is never told because eviction is invisible. A per-recipient cap makes the cost of an unreachable conversation land on that conversation. It also matches bitchat's maxMessagesPerPeer.
