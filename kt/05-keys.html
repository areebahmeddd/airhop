In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Imagine a padlock you can copy as many times as you like, and one single key that opens all the copies. You hand out open padlocks to everyone: to your friends, to strangers, you leave them lying in the street. Anyone can snap one shut on a box and send it to you, and nobody, not even the person who locked it, can open it again. Only you can, because only you have the key. The padlock is your public key. The key is your private key. The whole of modern security is that one idea plus the fact that nobody can build the key by looking at the padlock.

One-way functions

Everything in this chapter rests on one idea: some operations are easy to do and infeasible to undo. 

  EASY FORWARD, HARD BACKWARD

    multiply         7919 x 6841 = 54,173,879           instant
    factorise        54,173,879 = ? x ?                 slow

    that is a toy. Use 300-digit numbers and the second
    direction takes longer than the universe has existed.

  A TRAPDOOR one-way function is the same, with a shortcut:

    hard backward FOR EVERYONE
    easy backward IF YOU KNOW ONE SECRET

    the secret is the trapdoor. It is your PRIVATE KEY.

"Infeasible" is a real number, not a hand wave

128-bit security means roughly 2128 operations to break, which is about 340 undecillion. If every computer on Earth tried a trillion combinations per second, it would still take vastly longer than the age of the universe. Curve25519, ChaCha20 and SHA-256 all target that level.

Which is why real attacks are never against the mathematics. They are against implementations, key storage, human trust decisions, and metadata. Every finding in chapter 49 is one of those four, and none is a broken cipher.

Hashing

The simplest one-way function, and the most used.

  A HASH TAKES ANY INPUT AND PRODUCES A FIXED-SIZE OUTPUT

    SHA-256("hello")   -> 2cf24dba5fb0a30e26e83b2ac5b9e29e
                          1b161e5c1fa7425e73043362938b9824
    SHA-256("hellp")   -> 7d1a54127b222502f5b79b5fb0803061
                          152a44f92b37e23c6527baf665d4da9a

  ONE CHARACTER CHANGED. Completely different output. That is the
  AVALANCHE property, and it is what makes a hash a fingerprint.
PropertyMeansAirhop relies on it for
DeterministicSame input, same output, on every device forever.Safety numbers matching on two phones. Peer IDs agreeing everywhere.
Fixed sizeA one-byte input and a one-gigabyte input both give 32 bytes.Packet IDs.
Preimage resistantGiven an output, you cannot find an input that produces it.Peer IDs. An attacker cannot build a key that hashes to your ID.
Collision resistantYou cannot find two inputs with the same output.Packet deduplication being meaningful.
AvalancheOne bit changed, half the output bits change.Any tampering being obvious.

Preimage and collision resistance are different, and the difference matters

A peer ID is 8 bytes, so 64 bits. Finding a key that hashes to your specific ID takes about 264 work, which is out of reach. Finding any two keys that collide takes about 232 by the birthday bound, which is easy. But both would be the attacker's own keys, and colliding with yourself impersonates nobody. The property Airhop needs is preimage resistance, and 64 bits delivers it comfortably. That is why the ID is short and why that is fine.

Two variations you will meet constantly. HMAC is a keyed hash: HMAC(key, message) proves whoever made it knew the key. It exists because SHA-256(key || message) is not safe, since an attacker who sees the output can append data and produce a valid output for the longer message without knowing the key (length extension). HKDF turns one messy secret, like the raw output of a key agreement, into any number of clean independent keys.

Symmetric encryption

One key, used for both locking and unlocking.

  encrypt(key, plaintext)  -> ciphertext
  decrypt(key, ciphertext) -> plaintext

  SAME KEY BOTH TIMES.

  A STREAM CIPHER works by generating a keystream and XORing:

     plaintext   0110 1001
     keystream   1011 0011      <- from the key, looks random
     XOR         ────────
     ciphertext  1101 1010

  XOR is its own inverse, so the receiver runs the same keystream
  over the ciphertext and gets the plaintext back.

Two ways to destroy a stream cipher

Reuse the keystream. If two messages are encrypted with the same key and the same starting position, XORing the two ciphertexts together cancels the keystream entirely and leaves plaintext1 XOR plaintext2, which is often enough to recover both. This is why the nonce exists and why reusing a (key, nonce) pair is catastrophic rather than merely weak.

Forget to authenticate. XOR is transparent to an attacker: flip a bit in the ciphertext and the same bit flips in the plaintext. Without an authentication tag, an attacker who cannot read your message can still change it in targeted ways. That is why everything modern is AEAD: encryption and authentication as one inseparable operation, where a failed tag returns nothing rather than garbage.

Symmetric encryption is fast, simple and well understood. It has exactly one problem, and it is a big one.

The key distribution problem

  Alice wants to send Bob a secret.
  They agree to use symmetric encryption.

  First they must both have the same key.

  How does Alice get the key to Bob?

    send it over the network?   an eavesdropper takes it
    post it?                    slow, interceptable
    meet in person?             does not scale, and often impossible
    use a third party?          now the third party has your key

  AND WITH n PEOPLE YOU NEED n(n-1)/2 KEYS:

     10 people   ->     45 keys
    100 people   ->  4,950 keys
   1000 people   -> 499,500 keys

This was an unsolved problem for the entire history of cryptography until 1976. It is why codebooks were carried by hand, why embassies used diplomatic bags, and why a captured key compromised everything.

For Airhop it is fatal in a specific way: two strangers who have never met, with no internet, must agree a secret. There is no prior meeting, no trusted third party reachable, and no out-of-band channel. Symmetric cryptography alone cannot do it.

What a key pair actually is

The 1976 answer: use two keys with a mathematical relationship, where one can be public. 

  THE CURVE25519 VERSION, WHICH IS WHAT AIRHOP USES

  Start with a fixed, publicly known point on a curve. Call it G.
  Everyone in the world uses the same G.

     PRIVATE KEY   d      = 32 random bytes. A number.
     PUBLIC KEY    P      = d added to itself d times, on the curve
                          = "d times G", written d·G

  "Adding a point to itself d times" is EASY even for enormous d,
  because you can double repeatedly: G, 2G, 4G, 8G... and combine.
  About 256 doublings covers every possible d.

  GOING BACKWARDS is the ELLIPTIC CURVE DISCRETE LOGARITHM PROBLEM:
  given G and P, find d. Nobody knows how to do this faster than
  brute force, and brute force is 2^128 operations.

  SO A PUBLIC KEY IS LITERALLY A POINT ON A CURVE,
  AND A PRIVATE KEY IS LITERALLY A BIG RANDOM NUMBER.

What "elliptic curve" means, without the algebra

A curve is the set of points satisfying an equation. Curve cryptography defines a way to "add" two points and get a third point that is also on the curve. That addition has the useful property of being easy to do repeatedly and impossible to reverse.

You do not need the algebra to work on this codebase. What you need is: a public key is a point, a private key is a number, going forward is fast, going backward is infeasible, and 32 bytes of elliptic curve gives the same security as 384 bytes of RSA. That size difference is precisely why a Curve25519 public key fits in a 31-byte Bluetooth advertisement's neighbourhood and an RSA key does not.

Key agreement

Two people, each with a key pair, end up holding the same secret without ever transmitting it. This is Diffie-Hellman, and it is the single most important trick in the chapter. 

  ALICE                                            BOB
  ─────                                            ───
  a = random 32 bytes                              b = random 32 bytes
  A = a·G                                          B = b·G

              ─────────── A ──────────▶
              ◀────────── B ───────────
              (both public, sent in the clear)

  shared = a·B                                     shared = b·A
         = a·(b·G)                                        = b·(a·G)
         = (a·b)·G                                        = (a·b)·G

                  IDENTICAL 32 BYTES

  AN EAVESDROPPER HAS A AND B.
  To compute (a·b)·G they would need a or b, which means solving the
  discrete logarithm. They cannot.

The paint-mixing analogy, and where it breaks

The usual explanation is public paint plus a secret colour each, exchanged and mixed, arriving at the same final colour. It is a good intuition for why it works and it hides the thing that matters most:

Plain Diffie-Hellman has no idea who you are talking to. An attacker in the middle can run it twice, once with each of you, and hold two different shared secrets while relaying everything. Both of you believe you have a secure channel, and both of you are talking to the attacker.

Fixing that is exactly what Noise XX does: it performs three Diffie-Hellman operations, and two of them use the parties' long-term keys, so completing the handshake proves each side holds the private half of the identity it claims. Chapter 17.

One detail that matters in practice: the raw output of a key agreement is a curve coordinate, not a key. It has mathematical structure and is not uniformly random. Every protocol runs it through a KDF before using it, which is what mixKey does inside Noise and what kdfRk does inside the Double Ratchet.

Signing and verifying

The mirror image. Instead of privacy, it gives proof.

  SIGN                                      VERIFY
  ────                                      ──────
  sig = sign(message, PRIVATE key)          ok = verify(sig, message, PUBLIC key)

  ONLY the holder of the private key         ANYONE with the public key
  can produce a valid signature.             can check it.

  WHAT A SIGNATURE PROVES

    1. AUTHENTICITY   the holder of that private key made this
    2. INTEGRITY      not one bit has changed since
    3. NON-REPUDIATION they cannot later deny it

  WHAT IT DOES NOT DO

    it does not hide the message. A signature is attached to
    PLAINTEXT. Signing is not encrypting.
OperationUses which keyGives
Encrypt to someoneTheir publicOnly they can read it
DecryptYour privateYou read what was sent to you
SignYour privateEveryone knows it was you
VerifyTheir publicYou know it was them

Airhop signs almost everything that travels in the open: announces, public and private channel messages, attachments, voice frames, board posts and gateway uplinks. A relay that carries your packet cannot change a byte of it, and cannot invent one attributed to you.

Where Airhop deliberately does NOT sign, and why

Non-repudiation is usually described as a benefit. For private messaging it is a liability: it produces a cryptographic proof, that the recipient holds, that you said a specific thing. Airhop wants the opposite.

So messages inside a Noise session carry no signature. Their authenticity comes from a symmetric key both parties hold, so the recipient knows it came from you and cannot prove that to anybody else, because they could have produced it themselves. That property is called deniability, and it is why the Nostr rumor layer is unsigned too.

  SO EVERY REAL SYSTEM DOES THIS

    1. use ASYMMETRIC to agree on a symmetric key   (slow, once)
    2. use SYMMETRIC for all the actual data        (fast, forever)

  AIRHOP, CONCRETELY

    Noise XX handshake      3 messages, 3 elliptic curve operations
        ▼

        ▼

This is why a handshake is a few hundred milliseconds and a message is free. It is also why the Double Ratchet's design is clever: the expensive asymmetric step happens on each reply, and the cheap symmetric step happens on each message.

Randomness

This is where real systems break

A private key is only random bytes. If the randomness is predictable, the key is guessable, and every other layer is irrelevant.

Historical failureWhat happened
Debian OpenSSL, 2008A patch removed the entropy source. Every key generated for two years came from a pool of 32,768 possibilities.
PlayStation 3, 2010Sony reused the same ECDSA nonce for every signature. That alone reveals the private key algebraically. The console's code signing was permanently broken.
Android Bitcoin wallets, 2013A weak SecureRandom produced repeated nonces. Wallets were drained.
Dual_EC_DRBGA standardised random generator with a structure that permitted a backdoor for whoever chose its constants.

The rule is: never write your own, always use the operating system's. Airhop calls crypto.getRandomValues, which is SecRandomCopyBytes on iOS and the Android framework's CSPRNG underneath. Every private key, ephemeral key, nonce, stream ID and message ID comes from it.

The one line holding this up

Hermes, React Native's JavaScript engine, does not implement crypto.getRandomValues. A polyfill provides it, and its import sits at the very top of src/app/app.tsx with a comment saying it must be first. If any cryptography module were imported before it, key generation would fail at runtime. That single import line is load-bearing for the entire security model.

One elegant sidestep worth knowing: Ed25519 is deterministic. Its per-signature nonce is derived by hashing the private key together with the message, rather than being drawn from the random generator. So signing is safe even on a device whose randomness is broken, which is precisely the failure that destroyed ECDSA in the two cases above. It also means the same message signed twice produces identical bytes, which keeps Airhop's content-derived packet IDs stable.

The part cryptography cannot solve

  Cryptography can prove:

     "the holder of private key K signed this"          ✓ certain
     "only the holder of private key K can read this"   ✓ certain

  Cryptography CANNOT prove:

     "private key K belongs to the person you mean"     ✗ NOT A MATHS PROBLEM

That last line is the whole of chapter 20, and every system answers it differently.

ApproachHowWeakness
Certificate authority (TLS, the web)A trusted third party signs "this key belongs to this name".You must trust hundreds of authorities. Any one of them can lie. Needs to be reachable.
Web of trust (PGP)People sign each other's keys, and you trust transitively.Nobody ever understood it. Effectively dead.
Trust on first use (SSH, Airhop)Accept the first key you see, then refuse any change.Wrong if an attacker is present at first contact.
In-person verification (Airhop, Signal)Scan a QR code, or read a safety number aloud.Requires effort, and most people skip it.
The key IS the identity (Airhop, Nostr, Bitcoin)There is no separate name to bind to. The key is the name.No human-meaningful names. Losing the key loses the identity, permanently.

Airhop uses the last three together. The key is the identity, because a peer ID is a hash of the public key. Trust on first use pins whatever you hear first. And an in-person QR scan is the only thing permitted to correct a pin, with the safety number as the route for somebody you can only reach by voice.

Drills

  • What is a public key, physically? A point on an elliptic curve, encoded as 32 bytes. It is produced by taking a fixed public starting point that everyone shares and adding it to itself a secret number of times, where that secret number is the private key. Going forward is fast because you can double repeatedly. Going backward, recovering the number from the point, is the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem, and nobody knows how to do it faster than 2128 brute force.
  • Explain Diffie-Hellman, then say what is wrong with it. Each side picks a secret number and publishes that number times the shared base point. Each then multiplies the other's public point by their own secret, and both arrive at the same value because multiplication commutes. An eavesdropper sees both public points and would need to solve a discrete logarithm to get further. What is wrong with it is that it authenticates nobody: an attacker in the middle runs it twice, once with each party, and relays everything while holding both secrets. Both sides believe they are secure. Fixing that requires binding the exchange to long-term identity keys, which is what Noise XX does with its es and se operations.
  • Why does every real system use both symmetric and asymmetric crypto? Asymmetric solves key distribution and proves identity but is slow. Symmetric is orders of magnitude faster but cannot get the key to the other party. So you use asymmetric once to agree a symmetric key, then symmetric for all the data. In Airhop that is a three-message Noise handshake producing two ChaCha20 keys, after which every message is cheap, with the Double Ratchet adding one curve operation per reply and one HMAC per message.
  • Why does Airhop deliberately not sign private messages? Because a signature is non-repudiable: it produces a permanent cryptographic proof, held by the recipient, that you said a specific thing. For private messaging that is a liability rather than a feature. Inside a Noise session, authenticity comes instead from a symmetric key both parties hold, so the recipient knows it came from you and cannot prove it to anyone else, because they could have produced the same bytes. That is deniability, and it is also why the Nostr rumor layer is unsigned.
  • Where do real cryptographic systems actually break? Almost never in the mathematics. They break in randomness (Debian's OpenSSL, the PlayStation 3's reused nonce, Android's weak SecureRandom), in implementation (timing side channels, a decoder that throws instead of returning null), in key storage, in metadata that the encryption never covered, and in the human step of deciding that a key belongs to a person. Every finding in this project's own review is one of those, and none is a broken cipher.
  • What can cryptography never prove? That a key belongs to the person you mean. It can prove the holder of key K signed something, and that only the holder of K can read something, both with certainty. Binding K to a human being is not a mathematical problem, and every system answers it socially: certificate authorities, a web of trust, trust on first use, in-person verification, or declaring that the key simply is the identity. Airhop uses the last three together.
