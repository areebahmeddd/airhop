Chapter 27 · The internet layer

Gateway and bridge

Two features that both carry traffic between the mesh and the internet, in opposite directions and for opposite reasons. Both are off by default. Understanding why they are separate is most of the chapter.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

The gateway is lending your phone signal to someone standing next to you who has none, so they can read the local noticeboard on the internet. The bridge is putting a walkie-talkie in two rooms that cannot hear each other, and having the internet carry what each room says to the other, so it feels like one room.

The carrier packet

Both features share one packet type, 0x28 NOSTR_CARRIER, byte-compatible with bitchat's NostrCarrierPacket. 

  TLV, 2-byte big-endian lengths (event JSON exceeds the 1-byte range)

    0x01  direction   1 byte     0x01 toGateway   0x02 fromGateway
                                 0x03 toBridge    0x04 fromBridge
    0x02  geohash     1 to 12 bytes UTF-8
    0x03  eventJSON   1 to 16384 bytes, a COMPLETE SIGNED Nostr event

  toGateway / toBridge   ride a DIRECTED packet, recipientID = the gateway
  fromGateway / fromBridge ride a BROADCAST packet

Why the carrier adds no encryption

The carried event is public geohash chat, which is already plaintext on Nostr. Encrypting it to the gateway would hide it from the mesh and reveal it to the gateway, which is backwards.

What it is is Schnorr-signed by the originator's per-cell identity. So a gateway cannot forge an event, cannot alter one, and cannot attribute one to somebody else. Every receiver verifies the carried event's own signature before acting on it, which is what makes it safe to accept an event that arrived through an untrusted intermediary.

The gateway republishes the event exactly as signed. It does not re-sign, cannot alter, and is not the author. That is the property that makes it safe to accept help from a stranger's phone.

Three things that make it work

MechanismDetail
The content-stable ID Both the radio copy and the bridged copy key the timeline row on the same identifier, derived from sender, timestamp and content (bridgeStableID here, MeshMessageIdentity.stableID in bitchat). So duplicates collapse in either arrival order, with none of bitchat's alias-removal bookkeeping. This is also why #bluetooth needs no message ID in its payload.
The unlinkable per-cell identity The bridge publishes under a geohash-derived key, not the operator's durable Nostr identity. So bridging does not tell relays who is running it.
The rendezvous cell Geohash precision 6, about 1.2 km, matching bitchat's BridgeService.Limits.cellPrecision. Two islands in the same cell meet. A device with no location of its own can use a cell advertised by a reachable bridge peer in ANNOUNCE TLV 0x06.

The scope limitation

The rendezvous covers the device's own geohash-6 cell only. Two islands 200 metres apart but on opposite sides of a cell boundary do not meet. Subscribing to the eight neighbouring cells would fix it and is a documented follow-up, not implemented. This is a real and easily encountered limitation: cell boundaries do not follow streets.

Mesh-only peers with no internet reach the rendezvous through a bridge peer using toBridge and fromBridge carriers, the same mechanism as the gateway. A single phone can be both a bridge and a gateway, which is the setup that actually happens; scenario N08 holds across a nine-seed sweep that the far island receives the message and the phone carrying it keeps its own copy.

Presence across the bridge

A bridge also publishes presence heartbeats to the rendezvous cell, at least 30 seconds apart, so the UI can report how many people are across the bridge. A participant counts for 10 minutes after their last heartbeat.

Loop prevention

Both features move traffic between two networks, so a message can come back to where it started. Both use the same three-cache pattern. 

  THE LOOP THAT MUST NOT HAPPEN

    C posts on the radio in island 1
      -> B bridges it to Nostr
        -> B receives its own event back from the relay
          -> B injects it into the local #bluetooth timeline
            -> B sees a new local message
              -> B bridges it to Nostr
                -> ... forever, at relay speed

  THREE CACHES, EACH CLOSING ONE EDGE

    publishedEventIDs     our own published events. Ignore them coming back.
    receivedEventIDs      acted on once. Never act twice.
    rebroadcastEventIDs   ferried to the mesh once. Never ferry twice.
    seenRadioStableIDs    a radio copy already exists. Do not bridge the
                          bridged copy back out.

  Each set is capped at 512 entries, oldest evicted.

The gateway uses the equivalent three caches for carrier traffic. Scenario N03 pins the multi-gateway case: two gateways in one room do not each publish the same message.

What each one costs the operator

Running a gateway

  • Your IP is used to publish other people's events. To a relay, their traffic looks like yours.
  • Your battery and data allowance carry someone else's traffic.
  • Your radio spends airtime ferrying inbound events to the mesh.
  • You announce capability bit 2, so everyone nearby knows you have internet.

What you do not take on: authorship. Every event stays signed by its originator, so you cannot be mistaken for the author, and you cannot alter what you carry.

Running a bridge

  • Your public room becomes reachable from anyone in the same 1.2 km cell with internet, including people not physically present.
  • You publish under an unlinkable per-cell key, so relays do not learn who you are, but they do learn that someone is bridging that cell.
  • You announce capability bit 7 and the rendezvous cell in TLV 0x06, so nearby peers know both.

The room changes character: #bluetooth stops being strictly "people I could walk to". That is why it is off by default and why the toggle lives on the room rather than in a settings screen nobody reads.

Media is never bridged

Attachments ride the BLE file-transfer path only. They are never published to Nostr, so a photo posted in #bluetooth reaches the local island and nothing else. Scenario N07 pins the related rule: a message marked nearby-only is never bridged.

Drills

  • Why does a gateway not sign the events it publishes? Because the event is already signed by its author's per-cell identity, and republishing it as-is is what makes the gateway safe to use. If the gateway signed, it would become the author: it could alter content, it could be blamed for what it carries, and a receiver would have no way to attribute a message to the person who actually wrote it. Verifying the carried event's own signature before acting is the receiver's half of the same property.
  • Explain the three loop caches. A bridge moves messages between two networks, so a message can return to where it started and be re-bridged forever. publishedEventIDs stops us acting on our own event coming back from a relay. receivedEventIDs stops acting twice on the same inbound event. rebroadcastEventIDs stops ferrying the same event onto the mesh twice. And seenRadioStableIDs stops bridging out a message that already exists as a radio copy. Each closes one edge of the cycle, and each is capped at 512 entries.
  • Why are both features off by default? Because both spend the operator's resources on other people and change what their own device reveals. A gateway lends your IP, battery and airtime, and announces that you have internet. A bridge changes the meaning of the local public room from "people I could walk to" to "anyone in this 1.2 km cell with a connection". Neither is a bad thing to do; both are decisions the user should make deliberately rather than discover.
  • Why does the bridge use a per-cell identity rather than the operator's Nostr key? Because the durable Nostr key is published in every ANNOUNCE and used for DMs, so bridging under it would tell every relay serving that cell exactly who is operating the bridge, and would link the operator's DM identity to a physical place. The per-cell key is derived by HMAC from a device seed and is unlinkable both to the durable identity and to any other cell.
  • What is the weakest part of these two features? Two things. The bridge covers one geohash-6 cell only, so two islands a few hundred metres apart on opposite sides of a boundary never meet, and cell boundaries do not follow streets. And gateway recovery after losing signal has no asserted deadline: the scenario records the recovery leg but does not bound it, so how long a gateway takes to become useful again is unmeasured.
