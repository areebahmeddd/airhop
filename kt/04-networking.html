Networking from scratch

Airhop exists because the normal way computers talk has a set of hidden dependencies. To understand what it removes, you first have to see what is normally there. This chapter builds the internet from nothing, then shows exactly which parts Airhop keeps.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Sending a message on the internet is like posting a letter, except the postal system has several separate jobs. One part writes the address on the envelope. One part decides which roads the van takes. One part checks the letter arrived and asks again if it did not. And one part looks up a person's street address when all you know is their name. Each job is done by a different set of rules, and they are stacked on top of each other. Airhop keeps the top job, the actual letter, and throws away almost everything below it.

Networking is built in layers. Each layer only talks to the one directly above and below it, and each one adds its own header to the front of whatever it was handed.

The insight worth carrying forward

Airhop's mesh is layer 2 plus layer 7, with nothing in between. There is no IP address for a peer, no port to connect to, and no routing table anywhere. A peer ID is not an address in the network sense: it is a name, and delivery works by flooding rather than by routing. That is why the whole system survives when "the internet" is gone, because it was never using the internet's layers in the first place.

Where this shows up in Airhop

Bluetooth GATT is closer to TCP in behaviour: a link is established, and writes on a given link arrive in order. The simulation's radio fabric encodes exactly that, with reordering off by default because GATT does not do it, while loss, duplication and corruption are modelled and also off by default.

But GATT is unreliable in a way TCP is not: a write can simply be refused when the queue is full, which is why Airhop's fragment sender backs off 60 ms and retries rather than treating a refusal as a dead link. The mesh above that behaves like UDP: packets may be lost, duplicated by multiple paths, and arrive out of order, which is why there is a deduplicator and why the Noise transport carries a sliding replay window that tolerates 1024 messages of reordering.

DNS, and what it costs you

You type a name. Something has to turn it into an IP address. That something is DNS, and it is a global, hierarchical, mostly plaintext lookup system. 

  YOU ASK FOR relay.damus.io

  1. your machine asks its RESOLVER (usually your ISP's, or 8.8.8.8)
  2. resolver asks a ROOT server:     "who handles .io?"
  3. resolver asks the .io server:    "who handles damus.io?"
  4. resolver asks damus.io's server: "what is relay.damus.io?"
  5. answer: 1.2.3.4, cached for a while (the TTL)

  FOUR PARTIES now know you asked for that name, at that time,
  from your IP address. Classic DNS is UNENCRYPTED UDP on port 53.

DNS is a surveillance and censorship chokepoint

  • Your ISP sees every name you look up. Even with HTTPS everywhere, the lookup itself is usually in the clear.
  • Blocking a name is the cheapest censorship there is. The resolver simply answers "no such name" or points somewhere else. This is the most common form of national-level blocking.
  • It is a dependency you did not choose. If DNS is down, everything is down, even though the servers are fine.

DNS over HTTPS and DNS over TLS encrypt the query, which hides it from the ISP and moves the same knowledge to whoever runs the encrypted resolver.

This is one of the three hidden dependencies chapter 01 names: a company's servers, reachable over an ISP's network, resolved by DNS. Airhop's Bluetooth path has no names to resolve, so DNS cannot break it, block it, or observe it. Its Nostr path does use DNS, which is one of several reasons that path is treated as the weaker one.

Airhop uses mDNS for real: it is how the LAN transport finds peers on a shared network. Chapter 29 is that transport, including what a published record exposes.

Airhop's designed and unshipped LAN transport

ARCHITECTURE.md specifies mDNS discovery on _airhop-mesh._tcp plus TCP links carrying the same packet frames Bluetooth carries. It is not implemented. Chapter 28 covers what it would buy and why it matters: it is the only proposed local transport that works iPhone to Android, because WiFi Aware is Android-only and Apple's equivalent cannot interoperate with it.

The three real constraints are also worth knowing, because they are why it is harder than it looks. Most guest and venue WiFi has client isolation, which blocks device-to-device traffic at the access point and cannot be detected before trying. mDNS itself is often filtered even where ordinary traffic works. And a TCP socket has no iOS background mode equivalent to bluetooth-central, so a locked iPhone drops the link.

  say which of the three devices it is for. There is nothing to
  translate it back to.

  THEREFORE: a phone behind NAT CANNOT BE A SERVER.

NAT is why peer-to-peer over the internet is hard

Two phones, each behind their own router, cannot simply connect to each other. Neither has a reachable address. The industry's answers are all workarounds: STUN (ask a server what your public address looks like), TURN (relay everything through a server), ICE (systematically try every combination), hole punching (both sides send outward at once and hope the routers open matching holes).

Every one of those needs a server. That is a large part of why Airhop's internet path is Nostr relays rather than direct peer-to-peer: a relay is a machine with a real address that both parties can reach outbound, which sidesteps NAT entirely.

And it is a large part of why Bluetooth is the foundation. Two phones in a room have no NAT between them, no addresses to translate, and no router to configure. The hardest problem in internet peer-to-peer simply does not exist there.

IPv6 has enough addresses that NAT is unnecessary, and every device can have a globally reachable address. Adoption is partial and uneven, so it does not solve the problem in practice. WiFi Aware sidesteps it differently: the peer's address is a link-local IPv6 delivered in the framework's network info, valid only on that direct radio link, which is why Airhop's WiFi transport needs Android API 29 even though discovery works at 26.

WebSocket is the right shape for Nostr because a relay must be able to push events to a subscribed client without being asked. Plain HTTP cannot do that: the client would have to poll, which is slow, wasteful, and would make a mesh gateway useless.

Why TLS certificates matter, and why Airhop cannot use them for the mesh

TLS proves you are talking to the real relay.damus.io because a certificate authority signed a statement to that effect, and your device ships with a list of authorities it trusts. That works because there is a name, a server, and a hierarchy of trusted third parties.

None of those exist on a Bluetooth mesh. There is no name to certify, no authority to ask, and no way to reach one offline. So Airhop authenticates by raw public key instead, which is exactly what Noise is designed for. Chapter 17.

One consequence of the relay URL rules in chapter 24 follows directly from this section: relay addresses are restricted to wss:// with a real DNS hostname, no bare IPs, and no .local or localhost. Allowing ws:// would mean permitting cleartext for the whole app on Android, because its network security config cannot scope an exception to an address range, and that same permission covers the mint URLs that carry bearer ecash.

What a "server" actually is

"No central server" is the project's fourth principle, so it is worth being precise about what is being refused. 

  A SERVER IS A MACHINE THAT

    1. has a reachable address (so it is not behind NAT)
    2. is always on (so it can be reached at any time)
    3. someone OWNS and PAYS FOR
    4. someone can therefore be COMPELLED to act on

  It is (3) and (4) that matter. The technical part is easy.
  The problem is that a machine somebody owns is a machine that can
  be subpoenaed, seized, taxed, blocked, or switched off.
ModelWho runs the serverWhat they can do
Centralised (WhatsApp, Telegram)One companyEverything. Log metadata, comply with orders, shut it down.
Federated (Matrix, Mastodon, email)Many operators, one per userYour operator sees your data and holds your identity. Better, still a server.
Relay (Nostr)Many, none of them yoursStore and forward signed blobs. No account, no identity held. Can drop, cannot forge or read.

Airhop is serverless on layer 2 and relay-based on layer 3, and it operates none of the relays. The chosen third parties are relays (many, interchangeable, hold no account) and mints (one at a time, chosen by the user, hold the money and nothing else). Both are named as trust boundaries rather than hidden.

The one-sentence summary

Airhop's offline path replaces addressing with names, routing with flooding, TLS with Noise, and servers with nothing, which is why the failures that stop every other messenger do not reach it.

Drills

  • A peer ID looks like an address. Is it one? No. It is a name, derived from a public key, and nothing routes on it. There is no table anywhere mapping peer IDs to locations, because no node knows the topology. A directed packet carries the recipient's ID and is flooded; every node relays it and only the addressee decrypts it. The only thing resembling routing is a direct-link shortcut when a peer has already been mapped to a specific radio link, and that is an optimisation, not the delivery mechanism.
  • Explain the difference between DNS and mDNS to a junior engineer. Same question, opposite architecture. DNS asks a global hierarchy of servers, unicast on port 53, and the answer comes from infrastructure somebody operates, which means four parties learn what you looked up and blocking a name is trivial. mDNS multicasts the question to everyone on the local network on port 5353, and the device that owns the name answers for itself. No servers, no registrars, no configuration, and it never leaves the network segment. That is why it is the right discovery mechanism for a local mesh and useless for reaching anything on the internet.
  • Why is peer-to-peer over the internet hard, and why is it easy over Bluetooth? NAT. There are not enough IPv4 addresses, so home routers give devices private addresses that mean nothing outside, and translate outbound traffic through one shared public address. Inbound has nothing to translate back to, so a phone behind NAT cannot be a server, and two phones behind different routers cannot address each other. Every workaround (STUN, TURN, ICE, hole punching) needs a server. Over Bluetooth there are no IP addresses, no routers, and no translation, so the hardest problem in internet peer-to-peer simply does not exist.
  • Why does Airhop not use TLS on the mesh? TLS authenticates a server by certificate, which requires a name, a certificate authority, and a way to reach or pre-trust one. A mesh of strangers has no names to certify and no authority reachable offline. Self-signed certificates would reduce TLS to raw public key authentication with a large ASN.1 parser attached, which is attack surface for no gain. Noise does raw public key authentication directly, with no PKI, which is exactly the shape the problem has.
  • "The internet is down" is not one failure. Name three and say which ones Airhop survives. A power cut takes out towers and routers while handsets still show bars. A national shutdown cuts operator data, usually at the DNS and routing level. Congestion leaves everything up and saturated so requests time out. Airhop's Bluetooth path survives all three, because it is a local radio with no addressing, no routing, no names and no servers. Its Nostr path survives none of them, which is exactly why the internet layer is an extension rather than the foundation.
