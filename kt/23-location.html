Chapter 23 · Rooms and places

Location and geohash

Turning "where you are" into a chat room, without a map server, an API key, or your coordinates ever leaving the device. The whole feature is arithmetic on a short string, and the privacy properties come from choosing how much of that string to reveal.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Imagine cutting the world in half, then cutting your half in half, then again, over and over, writing down which side you were on each time. After a few cuts you have a short code, and everyone standing near you ends up with the same code. That code is the name of a chat room. Say fewer letters and the room covers a whole country. Say more letters and it covers your street, which is why the app refuses to tell anyone the longer version.

How a geohash works

A geohash turns a latitude and longitude into a short string, by repeatedly halving the world and recording which half you were in. 

  START WITH THE WHOLE WORLD

     longitude  -180 ......................... +180
     latitude    -90 ......................... +90

  ALTERNATE between longitude and latitude, one bit each:

     bit 1  longitude: is it in the upper half?     yes -> 1
     bit 2  latitude:  is it in the upper half?     no  -> 0
     bit 3  longitude: upper half of what remains?  yes -> 1
     bit 4  latitude:  upper half?                  yes -> 1
     bit 5  longitude:                              no  -> 0
     ...

  EVERY 5 BITS BECOME ONE CHARACTER, in base32:

     0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz

     note: no a, i, l or o. They are removed because they look
     like 4, 1, 1 and 0 when a human reads a code aloud.

  RESULT

     "u4pruydqqvj"

  THE KEY PROPERTY: a SHARED PREFIX means PHYSICAL PROXIMITY.

     u4pruydqqvj   and   u4pruydqqvm    are metres apart
     u4pru         is the cell containing both
     u4            is the region containing that

Why this is exactly right for a serverless app

Truncating a geohash is zooming out. So "which room am I in at city scale" is a string slice, not a lookup. No map tile server, no geocoding API, no third party who learns which part of the world is being looked at, and it works offline. The neighbour calculation in utils/geohash-grid.ts is pure arithmetic on the string for the same reason, and it is correct at the poles and the date line, which the classic neighbour lookup tables famously are not.

Degradation, not failure

With location permission denied, the named channels resolve to no cell and keep working over Bluetooth exactly as before. Location is an enhancement, never a requirement. A teleported cell carries a fixed geohash, so it stays live over the internet even with no fix at all.

One identity per cell

Why the main Nostr key must never sign a location message

That key is published in every ANNOUNCE and used for DMs and nutzaps. Reusing it here would let any relay operator, or anyone watching a geohash, tie a movement history to a durable identity, and correlate the block, city and region cells somebody posts in as one person. 

  seed = HMAC-SHA256(signingPrivKey, "airhop-geohash-seed-v1")

  privKey = HMAC-SHA256(seed, utf8(geohash) || u32_BE(i))
            for i = 0, 1, 2 ... until it is a valid secp256k1 scalar

  fallback: SHA-256(seed || label)   (essentially never reached)
PropertyConsequence
DeterministicYou keep a stable pseudonym within a cell across restarts, so people recognise you in the room you are in.
Per cellPosting in #city and #region are unlinkable to each other and to your durable identity.
Derived, not storedNothing extra to provision, back up or wipe. The seed is one-way from the signing key.
Not on the wireNothing depends on the derivation, only that each cell gets its own key, so bitchat and Airhop interoperate without sharing seeds. They could not anyway: the seed never leaves the device.

Display follows bitchat's convention: the self-asserted nickname with the last four hex characters of the pubkey appended, or anon#abcd with no nickname. Nicknames here are untrusted decoration; the pubkey is the identity.

One implementation detail worth knowing about, because it is a latent divergence on bitchat's side: the counter is big-endian in bitchat-iOS and Airhop, and little-endian in bitchat-Android. It only matters when i > 0, which essentially never happens, because a random 32-byte HMAC output is a valid scalar with overwhelming probability.

Converging on the same relays

The correctness rule that looks like an optimisation

Relays are chosen by distance from the cell centre, never from the user's own position. Every participant must independently arrive at the same relay set, or two people in opposite corners of one city cell publish to relays the other never subscribed to and never see each other, despite being in the same channel.

Ties break on URL rather than insertion order, for the same reason: many relays in the bundled directory share identical coordinates, so ties are common rather than exotic, and two clients with differently ordered directories would otherwise pick different sets. 

  closestRelaysToGeohash(gh, decodeCenter, count = 5):
    centre = decodeCenter(gh)
    sort every directory entry by haversine distance from centre,
      breaking ties on the URL string
    take the first `count`

  count = 5, matching bitchat's nostrGeoRelayCount, so both
  clients converge on the same rendezvous.

Custom relays merge on top: with geo-relay discovery on, the five nearest stay intact (because converging on them is what makes location channels interoperate with bitchat) and the user's up to five custom relays are appended. With discovery off, the custom set replaces it, trading interoperability for control. The client's per-call relay ceiling is sized to hold both halves, because capping back to the default five would trim from the tail and silently drop exactly the custom entries the user added.

One room, two transports

  A PUBLIC LOCATION CHANNEL EXISTS ON BOTH TRANSPORTS AT ONCE

  YOU POST IN #city
        │
        ├──▶ BLUETOOTH: packet type 0x51, flooded, ttl 5-7
        │       [chLen][channel][idLen][msgId][text]
        │       reaches anyone NEARBY, including people with NO INTERNET
        │       backfilled by gossip sync, bit 24, 15 minute window
        │
        └──▶ NOSTR: kind 20000, tagged  g=
                signed by the PER-CELL identity
                published to the 5 relays nearest the cell CENTRE
                carries  mid=  so the two copies COLLAPSE
                reaches everyone in the cell WITH internet

  A bitchat peer receives only the Nostr copy, because bitchat
  publishes its location channels to relays and never over
  Bluetooth. That is exactly why the Bluetooth copy needed its
  own packet type: under 0x02 it would ALSO render in their
  single mesh room, addressed to an audience its author never chose.
SituationWhat reaches you
In the cell, with internetBoth copies, collapsed into one bubble by the shared message ID.
In the cell, no internetThe Bluetooth copy from anyone nearby, plus anything a nearby gateway ferries in from relays.
Not in the cell, with internetThe Nostr copy only. You appear as teleported.
bitchat peer in the cellThe Nostr copy. The Bluetooth 0x51 is relayed by them and never displayed.

Joining replays up to one hour of recent traffic, capped at 200 events, so the room is not empty on arrival and a busy cell cannot flood a client on join. Kind 20000 is in Nostr's ephemeral range (20000 to 29999), which means relays forward to live subscribers and do not persist, so a location channel leaves no durable record on any relay. The hour of history is whatever a relay happens to be holding in a buffer, not a guarantee.

Presence, and the precision floor

A presence heartbeat is a kind 20001 event whose content is the geohash itself. Subscribers learn that a pubkey is in that cell, which is what populates the participant list.

The hard rule

Presence is broadcast at precision 2, 4 and 5 only. Never 6, never 7.

A heartbeat at precision 7 would announce your position to within a 153 metre block, on a public relay, continuously, every 40 to 80 seconds. That is a live location feed. The refusal is enforced in the presence module rather than left to callers, because a rule that depends on every call site remembering it is a rule that will be broken.

ConstantValueReason
Heartbeat interval40 to 80 s, jitteredRandomised so a fixed interval cannot fingerprint the sender. Matching bitchat-iOS.
Participant window5 minutesCovers several heartbeats, so one missed round does not drop somebody from the list.
Allowed precisions2, 4, 5Coarse enough that "somewhere in this city" is not a position.
Decorrelation delayApplied on joinSo opening a channel does not immediately emit a heartbeat that correlates with the join.
Event kind20001, ephemeralRelays do not persist it, so presence leaves no history.

Presence is also deliberately excluded from gossip sync. Rotating-ID presence is valid only inside its epoch, and syncing it would let a device that was never in radio range collect presence it could not otherwise have observed.

Teleport

Open any cell by typing its geohash, without being there. Scout a spot before you arrive, or follow an area from another country.

The t=teleport tag is an honesty mechanism rather than a restriction: bitchat lists the sender as teleported too, so a room can distinguish "someone standing here" from "someone reading from elsewhere". Without it, a remote participant would be indistinguishable from a local one, which matters when the room is being used to coordinate something physical.

Neighbours, without a map

"The next neighbourhood over" is a tap rather than a geohash somebody has to know, and it is computed entirely on the string. 

  A GEOHASH IS A GRID BY CONSTRUCTION

     geohashBounds(gh) -> { minLat, maxLat, minLon, maxLon }

     the cell's SIZE is (maxLat - minLat) and (maxLon - minLon),
     so stepping one cell north is just adding that latitude span
     to the centre and re-encoding.

  ┌───────┬───────┬───────┐
  │  NW   │   N   │  NE   │
  ├───────┼───────┼───────┤
  │   W   │  YOU  │   E   │
  ├───────┼───────┼───────┤
  │  SW   │   S   │  SE   │
  └───────┴───────┴───────┘

  NO MAP, NO TILE SERVER, NO API KEY, and nothing that tells a
  third party which part of the world is being looked at.
  Correct at the POLES and the DATE LINE, which the classic
  neighbour lookup tables famously are not.

This is also the mechanism that would extend the mesh bridge, which currently covers only the device's own geohash-6 cell. Subscribing to the eight neighbours is a documented follow-up and would fix the case where two islands a few hundred metres apart sit on opposite sides of a cell boundary. Chapter 27.

Place names

u4pruy is precise and unreadable. The UI shows a human-readable label beside it, matching how bitchat labels its location channels. 

  place-names-store

    1. take the geohash the app ALREADY KNOWS
    2. decode its CENTRE
    3. reverse-geocode that centre, once, best effort, device-side
    4. cache it forever, keyed on (geohash, device language)

  RAW COORDINATES NEVER LEAVE THE DEVICE. Only the cell's centre
  is geocoded, and that centre is derived from the geohash, not
  from the user's actual position.
PropertyDetail
Best effortIt needs a network round trip and a platform geocoder, and either can be unavailable. Every failure resolves to no name and the UI simply omits it.
Cached permanentlyA geohash cell maps to the same place forever, so a cell is never geocoded twice.
Keyed on languageThe OS geocoder answers in the device's language, not the app's. Both native APIs accept a locale and expo-location exposes neither, and a native shim for a room-header label is not worth it, so the cache keys on that language and re-resolves when it changes.
Wiped on panicCached names trace the places a user has been active in, so the store is in the wipe list.

The location cache in location-service has a five minute TTL and is module state, so the panic wipe clears it explicitly: the wipe does not restart the process, and the next geohash channel would otherwise resolve from the position the old identity observed.

The location pin

Sharing a point rather than joining a room. Entirely separate from geohash channels, and deliberately minimal. 

  NoisePayloadType 0x50, inside a Noise session. 19 BYTES, FIXED.

    [0]        u8      version = 1
    [1..4]     i32 BE  latitude,  MICRODEGREES (degrees x 1e6)
    [5..8]     i32 BE  longitude, microdegrees
    [9..10]    u16 BE  horizontal accuracy in metres, 0xFFFF unknown
    [11..18]   u64 BE  WHEN THE FIX WAS TAKEN, unix ms
DecisionReason
Microdegrees, not floats1e-6 degrees is about 11 cm, far finer than any phone fix, and an integer encodes identically everywhere a float's last bits do not.
The timestamp is the FIX, not the sendA pin that waited in a composer or crossed several hops is older than the message carrying it, and the receiver has to be able to say so.
Never couriered, never gossipedA position delivered six hours later by a passing carrier points at where somebody used to be. A pin with no session to carry it is refused outright rather than queued.
Coordinates are refused, not clampedA sender chooses these bytes, and clamping an out-of-range value would turn nonsense into a plausible point somebody would then walk towards.
Sent once, on an explicit tapTo one contact. Never automatic, never a reply in kind, and never forwarded onward by the app.
19 bytesNever approaches a fragment boundary.

The threat model lists it as explicitly out of scope in one respect: a pin carries real coordinates, and although it is encrypted inside the recipient's Noise session so no relay can read it, the recipient holds it and can do what they like with it, including screenshotting it. There is no live sharing and no map, deliberately.

The honest summary

Location channels are the feature that leaks the most, and they leak it to relay operators rather than to peers. The defences are real (coarse cells, per-cell identities, ephemeral events, jittered heartbeats, no raw coordinates) and they do not add up to anonymity. Someone running a relay serving a cell can see how many pseudonyms are active there and roughly when.

Which is why the whole feature is opt in per channel, why location permission is requested only when a location channel is opened rather than at startup, and why the named channels degrade to Bluetooth-only rather than demanding a fix.

Drills

  • Why does a geohash make a good channel name? Because a shared prefix means physical proximity, so truncating the string is zooming out. "Which room am I in at city scale" is a string slice rather than a lookup, which means no map server, no geocoding API, no API key, no third party learning which part of the world is being examined, and it works entirely offline. Neighbour calculation is arithmetic on the same string, correct at the poles and the date line where the classic lookup tables are not.
  • Why must relays be chosen by cell centre rather than by the user's position? Because publisher and subscriber must independently arrive at the same relay set or the channel silently drops traffic. Two people in opposite corners of one city cell would pick different nearest relays if selection used their own positions, publish to relays the other never subscribed to, and never see each other despite being in the same room. Ties break on URL rather than insertion order for the same reason, since many directory entries share coordinates and ties are common.
  • Why is presence forbidden at precision 6 and 7? Because a heartbeat at precision 7 announces your position to within a 153 metre block, on a public relay, every 40 to 80 seconds, for as long as the channel is open. That is a live location feed rather than a room membership signal. Precisions 2, 4 and 5 are coarse enough that "somewhere in this city" is not a position. The restriction is enforced inside the presence module rather than left to callers, because a rule depending on every call site remembering it will eventually be broken.
  • Why does each cell get its own key? Because the main Nostr key is published in every announce and used for DMs and payments. Reusing it in location channels would let any relay operator, or anyone watching a geohash, tie a movement history to a durable identity and correlate the block, city and region cells someone posts in as the same person. A per-cell key derived by HMAC from a device seed is deterministic, so you keep a stable pseudonym within a room, and unlinkable, so two rooms cannot be joined up.
  • A location pin arrives six hours late. What should happen, and why? It should not arrive at all. A pin is never couriered and never gossiped, because a position delivered hours later points at where somebody used to be, which is worse than no information because it looks current. A pin with no live session to carry it is refused outright rather than queued. The payload also carries the timestamp of the fix rather than the send, so even a pin delayed by a few hops can be shown with its real age.
  • What does a relay operator learn about someone in a location channel? That a particular pseudonymous pubkey is active in a particular cell, roughly when, and how long each of their messages is, since bitchat's cipher does not pad. Without Tor they also see the IP, which geolocates roughly. What they cannot learn is the person's durable identity, or that two of their cell pseudonyms belong to one person, because the per-cell derivation breaks that link. One subtler leak: the relay set is derived from the cell centre, so which relays a client connects to narrows its location before it posts anything at all.
