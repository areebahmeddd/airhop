Radio, from scratch

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

A radio is a torch that flashes in a colour your eyes cannot see. Your phone flashes patterns of that invisible light, and another phone nearby watches for the flashes and turns them back into words. Like a torch, it gets dimmer the further away you are, and walls and people block it. It is not magic and it does not go through the ground. It just goes until it is too dim to read.

The engineering version: a radio transmitter converts electrical energy into an electromagnetic field that propagates outward at the speed of light. A receiver a distance away extracts a tiny amount of that energy from its antenna and tries to recover the pattern that was impressed on it. Everything difficult in radio comes from three facts: the energy spreads out over an expanding sphere so it falls off fast, the medium is shared with everybody else, and the receiver has a noise floor below which it simply cannot tell signal from static.

What a radio wave actually is

An oscillating electric field creates an oscillating magnetic field, which creates an oscillating electric field, and the pair self-propagates through space. That is a Maxwell equation result and it is the same phenomenon as visible light, differing only in frequency.

PropertyDefinitionBluetooth at 2.4 GHz
Frequency (f)Oscillations per second, in hertz.2.4 billion per second.
Wavelength (λ)Distance one oscillation covers. λ = c / f, where c is 300,000,000 m/s.300,000,000 / 2,400,000,000 = 12.5 cm
AmplitudeHow strong the field is. This is what falls off with distance.Set by transmit power, typically 0 to 4 dBm on a phone.
PhaseWhere in the oscillation you are, from 0 to 360 degrees.The thing BLE actually modulates information onto.

That 12.5 cm wavelength is worth remembering, because it explains several otherwise strange behaviours. It is roughly the size of a human hand, which is why holding a phone a particular way changes reception. It is much smaller than a wall, which is why walls attenuate rather than diffract around. And an efficient antenna is usually a quarter or a half of a wavelength, so at 2.4 GHz an antenna can be about 3 cm, which is why it fits inside a phone at all.

The spectrum, and why 2.4 GHz

Radio spectrum is a finite, government-allocated resource. Most of it requires a licence. A few small slices are set aside as ISM bands (industrial, scientific and medical) where anyone may transmit without a licence, subject to power limits and behaviour rules. 2.4 GHz is the most important of these because it is unlicensed almost everywhere on Earth.

BandWavelengthBehaviourUsed by

mmWave, 28 GHz+1 cmEnormous capacity, blocked by a hand, range measured in tens of metres.5G mmWave.

Airhop uses 2.4 GHz because Bluetooth uses it, and Bluetooth uses it because that is the only band with three properties at once: legal worldwide without a licence, enough bandwidth for megabits, and a wavelength small enough for the antenna to fit in a phone. Every other choice would fail one of those.

There is a genuine trade being made. 868 MHz would roughly double the range for the same power, because free-space loss goes as the square of frequency. But no phone has an 868 MHz radio, and regional allocations differ (433 MHz in some regions, 868 in Europe, 915 in North America), so an app cannot use it without hardware. That is precisely why LoRa relay boxes exist as an addition to the phone mesh rather than a replacement.

Turning bits into a wave

A pure sine wave carries no information. To carry information, you change something about the wave over time. That is modulation.

  • Amplitude modulation: make it louder for a 1, quieter for a 0. Simple, and terrible in the presence of noise, because noise is amplitude.
  • Frequency modulation: shift slightly higher for a 1, slightly lower for a 0. Far more robust. This is what BLE uses, specifically GFSK (Gaussian frequency shift keying). The Gaussian filter smooths the transitions so the signal does not splatter energy into neighbouring channels.
  • Phase modulation: shift where in the cycle you are. Used by WiFi and by Bluetooth's higher-speed modes.
  • Spread spectrum: deliberately smear the signal across a wide band so narrow interference cannot destroy it. Bluetooth uses frequency hopping (see below); LoRa uses chirp spreading (chapter 47); WiFi uses OFDM across many subcarriers.

BLE 1 Mbps uses GFSK with a modulation index around 0.5, which means the frequency deviates by roughly 250 kHz either side of the channel centre. The receiver only has to decide "higher or lower", which is a much easier question than "how loud", and that is where BLE's robustness at low power comes from.

Power, dBm, and why the numbers are negative

Radio power spans an enormous range: a transmitter might emit 1 milliwatt and a receiver might detect 0.0000000001 milliwatts. Linear numbers are unusable at that scale, so everything is expressed in decibels relative to a milliwatt: dBm. 

  dBm = 10 x log10(power in milliwatts)

     1 W      = 1000 mW  =  +30 dBm     (a WiFi access point at max legal EIRP)
   100 mW     =            +20 dBm      (a strong WiFi client)
    10 mW     =            +10 dBm      (Bluetooth class 1)
     1 mW     =              0 dBm      (typical phone BLE transmit)
   0.1 mW     =            -10 dBm      (very close received signal)
  0.0000001 mW =           -70 dBm      (a normal received signal a room away)
  0.00000000001 mW =      -100 dBm      (about the noise floor)

The three numbers to memorise

  • +3 dB is double the power. -3 dB is half.
  • +10 dB is ten times. -10 dB is one tenth.
  • -6 dB is half the distance in free space, because power falls off with the square of distance.

From those three you can do most link budget arithmetic in your head. A phone at 0 dBm read at -60 dBm has lost 60 dB, which is a factor of one million.

RSSI (received signal strength indicator) is the received power in dBm. It is always negative in practice, because the received signal is always a tiny fraction of a milliwatt. Airhop polls it from the native module (every 5 to 60 seconds depending on the power mode) and uses it for two things: drawing distance rings on the mesh radar, and gating whether to open a GATT connection at all.

RSSIPractical meaning
-30 to -50 dBmIn the same hand, or a metre away. Excellent.
-50 to -70 dBmSame room. Reliable.
-70 to -85 dBmNext room, or across a large space. Usable, with retries.
-85 to -95 dBmMarginal. Connections drop, throughput collapses.
Below -95 dBmAt or below the noise floor. Not usable.

RSSI is not distance. It correlates with distance only when nothing is in the way, everything is oriented the same, and both radios have the same power. In a real crowd, none of those hold. A phone in a back pocket ten metres away can read weaker than a phone behind a wall at thirty. The radar draws rings from RSSI because it is the only signal available, and it is labelled as approximate for exactly this reason. This is also why Airhop's peer store deliberately refuses to treat a locally polled RSSI as evidence of liveness: only a packet that arrived proves the peer is there.

The link budget: the one equation that matters

Every radio question reduces to this. Add up everything that helps, subtract everything that hurts, and compare the result to what the receiver needs. 

  RX power (dBm) = TX power
                 + TX antenna gain
                 - path loss
                 - obstruction loss
                 - body loss
                 + RX antenna gain

  Link works if:  RX power  >  receiver sensitivity  (+ a margin)

  FREE SPACE PATH LOSS, the dominant term:

     FSPL(dB) = 20 x log10(d) + 20 x log10(f) - 147.55
                (d in metres, f in hertz)

  At 2.4 GHz that comes out as:

     1 m     40 dB          30 m     70 dB
     3 m     50 dB         100 m     80 dB
    10 m     60 dB         300 m     90 dB

Now do a real phone-to-phone link:

TermValueWhy

TX antenna gain0 dBiA phone antenna is roughly omnidirectional and small. Assume no gain.

That arithmetic is where every range figure in Airhop's documentation comes from. "10 to 30 m indoors, up to about 100 m line of sight" is not a marketing number, it is the range over which the margin above stays positive under realistic conditions.

Why walls and people ruin everything

Free space loss assumes nothing between the antennas. Reality subtracts more.

ObstructionLoss at 2.4 GHzEffect

Three further effects matter in the environments Airhop targets:

  • Multipath fading. A signal arrives by several paths (direct, plus bounces off walls and floors). Depending on path length differences those copies add or cancel. Cancellation can produce a 20 dB null in a spot the size of half a wavelength, which at 2.4 GHz is about 6 cm. This is why moving a phone a few centimetres can restore a link, and why frequency hopping helps: a null at one frequency is not a null at another.
  • The Fresnel zone. A radio link needs clearance around the direct line, not just an unobstructed line. The first Fresnel zone is an ellipsoid between the antennas; blocking more than about 40 percent of it causes real loss even if you can see from one antenna to the other. At 2.4 GHz over 100 m the zone radius at the midpoint is about 1.8 m, which is why a link across a street can degrade when someone parks a van halfway along it.
  • Ground reflection. Two phones held at waist height have a strong ground bounce that can partially cancel the direct path. Range measured with phones on the floor is systematically worse than range measured with phones held up.

The consequence for the mesh

Range is not a number, it is a distribution. This is the strongest argument for the flooding design: no node can know the topology, because the topology is changing continuously as people move, turn, and put phones in pockets. A routing table computed a second ago is already wrong. Flooding with a hop limit is robust to a topology nobody can measure. See chapter 11.

Antennas

An antenna is a transducer between a current in a wire and a field in space. Its two properties that matter here are gain and pattern, and they are the same property viewed two ways.

  • Gain is not amplification. An antenna is passive. Gain means it concentrates the same energy into a narrower direction, at the cost of the directions it no longer covers. A 12 dBi antenna does not create energy; it makes a beam.
  • dBi is gain relative to a theoretical isotropic radiator (equal in all directions). dBd is relative to a half-wave dipole, and dBi = dBd + 2.15.
  • Polarisation is the orientation of the electric field. Two antennas at 90 degrees to each other lose 20 dB or more. This is why phone orientation matters and why a phone lying flat on a table can be much worse than one held upright. Phones use small antennas with messy patterns partly to reduce this sensitivity.

For Airhop on phones there is nothing to tune: the antenna is whatever the manufacturer put in, and the app cannot influence it. This matters for relay hardware though (chapter 47), where a 3 dBi whip versus a 6 dBi collinear on a solar node is a real doubling of coverage area, and where getting the node a few metres higher usually beats any antenna change.

Sharing the band with everyone

The 2.4 GHz band is 83.5 MHz wide, from 2.400 to 2.4835 GHz, and it is the busiest piece of spectrum in the world.

Two design decisions in Bluetooth follow directly from this crowding:

  • The three advertising channels are placed deliberately. Channels 37, 38 and 39 sit in the gaps between the three non-overlapping WiFi channels. That is not a coincidence, it is coexistence engineering. It is also why a device advertises on all three in sequence: if one is blocked, the others may not be.
  • Adaptive frequency hopping. Once connected, a BLE link hops between 37 data channels many times per second, and both ends maintain a channel map that excludes channels found to be bad. A microwave oven that destroys 2450 MHz becomes a channel the link stops using.

The practical consequence for Airhop: a crowded room is harder than an empty field at the same distance. Not only because bodies absorb, but because everyone's phones, everyone's WiFi, and the venue's own equipment are all contending for the same 83.5 MHz. This is the single biggest reason for the relay jitter window and the fanout subsetting in chapter 11: airtime, not bandwidth, is the scarce resource.

Regulation

Unlicensed does not mean unregulated. Every region caps power and imposes behaviour rules, and equipment must be certified.

RegionRegulator2.4 GHz limit
United StatesFCC, Part 15.2471 W conducted, 4 W EIRP for point to multipoint.
EuropeETSI EN 300 328100 mW EIRP, with duty cycle and listen-before-talk rules.
JapanARIB / MIC10 mW per MHz.

None of this constrains Airhop as an app: the phone's radio is already certified, and the OS will not let an application exceed what the hardware is approved for. setPowerMode selects among the levels the platform exposes (HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW, ULTRA_LOW), all of which are within the certified envelope. It does constrain relay hardware, where sub-GHz band choice is region specific and duty cycle limits are real: in Europe, 868 MHz LoRa is capped at roughly 1 percent duty cycle in most sub-bands, which is a hard limit on how much a node may transmit per hour. Chapter 47 covers that.

Drills

  • Why does Airhop not just increase transmit power to get more range? Three reasons. The app cannot: transmit power is set by the platform within a certified envelope, and setPowerMode only picks among levels the OS exposes. Even at maximum, the gain is small: +10 dB of power buys roughly 3x the distance in free space and much less indoors. And it is not free, since transmit power is the dominant battery cost after scanning, and this app is meant to run in a pocket all day. More range comes from more hops, and more hops come from more people.
  • A user reports the app works across a car park but not through two office walls at half the distance. Explain. Free space loss at 60 m is about 76 dB. Two modern office walls could be 20 to 40 dB on their own, and if the glass is metal-coated it can be worse than the concrete. So 30 m through two walls can easily be a worse link than 60 m across open ground. Distance is only one term in the budget and rarely the dominant one indoors.
  • Why is 2.4 GHz both the right choice and the worst band? Right because it is the only globally unlicensed band with a wavelength small enough for a phone antenna and enough bandwidth for megabits, so every phone already has a radio for it. Worst because that same universality means WiFi, Zigbee, microwave ovens and every other phone in the room are contending for the same 83.5 MHz. Bluetooth's answer is adaptive frequency hopping plus advertising channels placed in the WiFi gaps.
  • Someone proposes showing distance in metres on the mesh radar from RSSI. What do you say? No. RSSI maps to distance only under free space with matched antennas and matched orientation, and none of those hold. A phone in a pocket at 10 m routinely reads weaker than a phone in a hand at 30 m. Showing metres would be a confident lie. Relative rings labelled as approximate are the honest presentation, and that is what the radar does. It is the same principle as reporting delivery state: never claim precision the system does not have.
  • What is the Fresnel zone and why would you mention it to someone deploying relay hardware? It is the ellipsoid around the direct path that needs to stay clear; blocking more than about 40 percent of the first zone costs real signal even with an unobstructed sightline. At 2.4 GHz over 100 m the midpoint radius is about 1.8 m. It is why raising a node two metres often does more than any antenna upgrade, and why a link that worked in winter can fail when trees leaf out.
