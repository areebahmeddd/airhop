Custody and the nutzap flow

"Is this a custodial wallet?" deserves a precise answer rather than a slogan, because the honest one is neither yes nor no. This chapter gives it, then traces a nutzap end to end and lists every NIP and NUT in play.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Think of a fairground. You swap real money for tokens at a booth, and then the tokens are yours: they live in your pocket, you can hand them to a friend without asking anyone, and nobody can take them out of your pocket. But the booth is still holding your real money, and if the booth burns down or the owner runs away, your tokens buy nothing. So the tokens are yours, and the money behind them is not.

The answer, precisely

Airhop is a non-custodial wallet holding claims against a custodian

Airhop the app is non-custodial in every sense. The project holds nothing, sees nothing, and cannot move, freeze or recover anyone's money. There is no account, no server, and no administrative key.

The mint is a custodian of the backing bitcoin. It holds the sats that the ecash represents. It can refuse redemption, inflate, or disappear.

The proofs are bearer instruments you hold. They sit encrypted on your device. The mint cannot spend them, cannot see them, and cannot connect the ones it issued to the ones it later redeems.

The one-sentence version for a user: the coins are yours, the money behind them is at the mint you chose.

What would be dishonest to say

  • "Non-custodial", full stop. That implies nobody can take your funds, and a mint absolutely can. Cashu is explicitly a minimal trust system, not a trustless one.
  • "Custodial". That implies a company holding an account for you that it could freeze or hand over. There is no account, and the mint does not know who you are.
  • "Your keys, your coins." True about the proofs, misleading about the value, because the value depends on a party honouring a redemption.

What is yours and what is theirs

Entirely yours

  • The proofs. On your device, in an AES-256 encrypted MMKV partition under a key in the OS keychain.
  • The secrets. Each proof's secret is known only to you. The mint has never seen it until you spend.
  • The P2PK key that nutzaps lock to.
  • The recovery phrase, if you enabled it.
  • The mint list. You choose. The app ships with none.
  • The transaction history. Local only. No NIP-60 events are published.
  • The decision to spend. No approval, no limits, no counterparty.

At the mint

  • The backing bitcoin. Real sats, on Lightning or on chain, held by the operator.
  • The signing keys that make a proof valid.
  • The spent set, which is the only thing that can answer "has this been spent".
  • The power to refuse redemption, or to inflate.

Why this is much better than it sounds

A custodial payment provider knows who you are, who you pay, when, and how much, and it holds the balance in an account it can freeze on request. A Cashu mint knows only that it issued N sats at some point and redeemed N sats at some other point, with no way to connect the two, and it has no account to freeze because there is nothing tying a proof to a person.

The custody risk is real and it is narrow: the mint can fail to honour redemption. It cannot surveil, cannot censor a specific user, and cannot selectively freeze.

Choosing a mint

The mint is the one trust decision the user makes, so the app tries to make it an informed one rather than a hidden one.

Design decisionEffect
No default mint shipsThe user must add one deliberately. There is no way to receive money without having made the trust decision first.
Balances are per (mint, unit), never pooledOne mint failing cannot take the rest. It is also why the balance screen refuses to show a single number: a token names exactly one mint, so value at two mints is not fungible without a Lightning round trip.
Incoming ecash is only redeemed at a known mintA nutzap naming a mint the wallet does not already hold is refused outright. This was a High severity finding when it was not enforced: it would let an attacker introduce a hostile mint through a payment.
Test mints are labelledA heuristic on the mint's own name, description and hostname (testnut, fakewallet, regtest, signet). Deliberately permissive, because a false positive costs a needless badge while a false negative lets somebody mistake play money for real sats. It only ever adds a warning and never blocks.
You can run your ownWhich converts the custody question into "do I trust myself".
Consolidation existsconsolidateMints moves value between mints over Lightning, so leaving a mint you no longer trust costs one routing fee rather than an external wallet.

The nutzap flow, traced

A nutzap is not a request to pay, it is the payment. NIP-61, two event kinds. 

  ─── STEP 0: THE RECIPIENT ADVERTISES, ONCE ───────────────────────

  Bob publishes kind 10019 (replaceable, so it has one current version)

     tags:  ["relay",  "wss://relay.example"]      where to send them
            ["mint",   "https://mint.example", "sat"]   what he accepts
            ["pubkey", "02a1b2...33 bytes hex"]    LOCK TO THIS

  If Bob never publishes this, he cannot receive nutzaps at all, and
  a payment to him degrades to an ordinary token in a DM.

  ─── STEP 1: THE SENDER PREPARES ──────────────────────────────────

  Alice: findNutzapTarget(bobPubkey)
     fetch Bob's kind 10019
     intersect HIS mint list with the mints ALICE ALREADY HOLDS VALUE AT
        no overlap -> CANNOT nutzap. Fall through to rail 3.
     pick the mint, in Bob's stated order of preference

  ─── STEP 2: LOCK THE PROOFS ──────────────────────────────────────

  Alice: lockProofsForNutzap({ amount, mint, p2pkPubkey })
     swap her ordinary proofs at the mint for new ones carrying a
     NUT-11 P2PK spending condition: only a signature from Bob's
     key can spend them.

     THIS IS THE COMMITMENT POINT. Past here the money is BOB'S,
     whether or not he ever comes online. It is NOT reclaimable.

  ─── STEP 3: PUBLISH ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  Alice: publishLockedNutzap(...)
     kind 9321
       content: an optional comment, max 280 chars
       tags:  ["proof", ]   ONE TAG PER PROOF, up to 64
              ["u", ]         which mint issued them
              ["p", ]
              ["e", , ]   optional, what is zapped

     PUBLISHED TO BOB'S RELAYS, from his kind 10019, NOT ours.
     That is where he subscribes.

  THE EVENT IS PUBLIC. Anyone can read the proofs. NOBODY BUT BOB
  CAN SPEND THEM, because of the P2PK lock. So the relay never
  holds spendable value.

  ─── STEP 4: THE RECIPIENT CLAIMS ─────────────────────────────────

  Bob's nutzap watcher is subscribed to kind 9321 with p=,
  with a 30 DAY LOOKBACK so nothing is missed while offline.

     for each event:
       verify the Schnorr signature
       is the "u" mint one BOB ALREADY HOLDS?   no -> REFUSE OUTRIGHT
       parse at most 64 proof tags, each at most 4096 bytes
       DLEQ-verify the proofs (NUT-12)
       SWAP them at the mint, signing with the P2PK private key,
         which converts them into ordinary proofs only Bob controls
       record it in local history

  ─── STEP 5: SETTLEMENT ───────────────────────────────────────────

  Alice: settleNutzap(txId) when it is confirmed delivered,
         failNutzapDelivery(txId, reason) if the relay refused.

     PayResult.final === true for this rail ALONE, and every
     confirmation names the rail and says whether Pending will
     offer the money back. For a nutzap it will not.
GuardValuePrevents
Proofs per nutzap64A single event forcing unbounded parsing work.
Proof tag length4096 bytesAn oversized tag exhausting memory.

Every field of a nutzap event is treated as hostile until parsed, because the event is public and unauthenticated apart from its signature, and the signature only proves who published it, not that the contents are sane.

P2PK, and the 33 byte trap

NUT-11 adds a spending condition to a proof: it can only be spent by presenting a signature from a named public key. 

  AN ORDINARY PROOF

     secret = random bytes
     spend it by presenting the secret

  A P2PK-LOCKED PROOF

     secret = a structured condition naming a PUBLIC KEY
     spend it by presenting a SIGNATURE from the matching private key

  So a locked proof can be PUBLISHED IN THE OPEN and remain
  unspendable by everyone except the key holder. That is what makes
  a public payment event safe.

The mistake that makes money unspendable by everyone

The pubkey tag in a kind 10019 is a 33 byte compressed secp256k1 key. A Nostr pubkey is a 32 byte x-only key. They are different encodings of related things, and locking to the wrong form produces proofs that nobody can spend, including the sender who created them. The money is not stolen, it is simply gone.

This is why Airhop keeps a separate P2PK key in the keychain under airhop.wallet.p2pk.v1 rather than reusing the Nostr identity key directly, and why the module's header comment names it as one of the two rules that are easy to get wrong and both lose money. The other is using a mint the recipient does not accept, which produces proofs that are valid and worthless to them.

PropertyConsequence
The recipient need not be onlineThe proofs wait in a public event until they subscribe. This is why the rail is final: the money is theirs already.
The relay holds no spendable valueA hostile relay operator reading every nutzap gains nothing.
The sender cannot reclaimThe proofs are locked to somebody else's key. There is no path back, which is exactly why PayResult.final is true only here.
Amounts are publicAnyone reading the event sees the value and the mint. Nutzaps are not private payments, they are unstealable public ones.

Why NIP-60 is not published

NIP-60 defines how to store a Cashu wallet's state on Nostr: kind 17375 for the wallet, kind 7375 for token events. It makes a wallet portable across devices without any local backup.

And it would publish your balance history to relays

Token events are encrypted to your own key, so a relay cannot read the amounts. But it can see that there are events, how many, how often, and how large, all tied to one durable pubkey. That is a transaction-frequency and rough-volume feed for anyone watching, indefinitely, on infrastructure the project does not operate.

Airhop keeps history local only, in the encrypted wallet partition, wiped by the panic wipe. The cost is that a wallet does not follow you to a new device automatically, which is exactly what the optional NUT-13 recovery phrase is for: it rebuilds the balance from the mint's records rather than from anything published anywhere.

This is a good illustration of the project's general posture: given a choice between convenience that publishes metadata and inconvenience that does not, it takes the inconvenience and makes the recovery path explicit and opt-in.

How money is actually lost

Not by cryptographic break. These are the real paths, in rough order of likelihood.

PathAirhop's handling
The phone is lost and backup was offMoney gone, like cash in a lost wallet. The recovery phrase is off by default and its coverage is shown honestly, including the uncovered remainder.
The mint disappearsValue at that mint is gone. Per-mint balances mean the rest survives. consolidateMints lets you leave a mint you stop trusting.
An offline token was already spentStored as unverified and never counted as settled. refreshAccount runs a NUT-07 check and removes anything the mint reports spent, rather than showing it as money.
A send is abandoned mid-flightProofs move to reserved rather than being deleted, and the serialised token stays on the transaction, so it is reclaimable. A crash, a closed sheet, or a DM that never routes all leave the value recoverable.
A swap response is lostThe blinded messages are persisted before the call, so the retry replays against the mint's NUT-19 cache, or recovers the outputs by NUT-09 using those exact blinded messages. Inputs are not removed until outputs are in hand.
A token posted in a public channelIt is a bearer instrument: anyone reading can redeem it. The UI states this before sending.
Reclaiming a send the recipient did receiveA race, not a cancellation: whoever reaches the mint first keeps it. The UI says so before reclaiming.
A nutzap locked to the wrong key formUnspendable by everyone. Prevented by using the 33 byte compressed P2PK key from the recipient's kind 10019.
A nutzap from a mint the recipient does not acceptValid proofs, worthless to them. Prevented by intersecting mint lists before locking anything.
An empty token handed overA real past bug: a failed proof mapping returned exact: true with an empty list. The invariant now is that a send either covers the amount or refuses.

Drills

  • Is Airhop a custodial wallet? Answer in three sentences. The app is non-custodial: it holds nothing, sees nothing, and cannot move, freeze or recover anyone's money, and there is no account anywhere. The proofs are bearer instruments held on your device, encrypted at rest, which the mint cannot spend, see, or link to you. But the mint is a custodian of the backing bitcoin and can refuse redemption or disappear, so the honest phrasing is that the coins are yours and the money behind them is at the mint you chose.
  • What exactly can and cannot a mint do? It can refuse to redeem, disappear with the backing, issue proofs it has no backing for, see your IP without Tor, and see its own totals issued and redeemed. It cannot spend your proofs, see your balance, link an issuance to a redemption (blind signatures break that), know who you paid (a transfer never touches it), freeze an account (there is none), or identify you (there is no identity). The custody risk is real and narrow: failure to honour redemption, not surveillance or selective censorship.
  • Trace a nutzap from intention to settlement. The recipient must first have published a kind 10019 listing their relays, the mints they accept, and a 33 byte P2PK pubkey. The sender fetches it, intersects the recipient's mint list with mints they already hold value at, and refuses the rail if there is no overlap. They swap their proofs for new ones P2PK-locked to the recipient's key, which is the commitment point: past there the money is the recipient's and is not reclaimable. They publish a kind 9321 to the recipient's relays with one proof tag per proof and the mint URL. The recipient's watcher, subscribed with a 30 day lookback, verifies the signature, refuses any mint it does not already hold, DLEQ-verifies the proofs, and swaps them using the P2PK private key into proofs only it controls.
  • Why is a nutzap the only payment rail reported as final? Because the proofs are cryptographically locked to the recipient's key by a NUT-11 spending condition. They are not ours to reclaim, and they are the recipient's whether or not they ever come online. Every other rail reserves proofs that can be released back to spendable. Conflating the two would either tell a user their money is safely delivered when it is sitting in a queue, or offer to take back money that is no longer theirs, which is why PayResult.final exists and why every confirmation names the rail.
  • Why does Airhop not publish NIP-60 wallet events? Because although the token events are encrypted to your own key, a relay still sees that events exist, how many, how often and how large, all tied to one durable pubkey. That is a transaction frequency and rough volume feed, published indefinitely on infrastructure the project does not run. History stays local in the encrypted wallet partition and is destroyed by the panic wipe. The cost is that a wallet does not follow you to a new device, which the optional NUT-13 phrase covers by rebuilding the balance from the mint's own records instead.
  • Somebody says "your keys, your coins" about this wallet. Correct them. It is true about the proofs and misleading about the value. You hold the proofs and the secrets, and nobody can take them from your device or spend them. But a proof is a claim against a mint, and the mint holds the bitcoin behind it. If it refuses redemption or disappears, you hold cryptographically perfect claims that buy nothing. Cashu is explicitly a minimal trust system rather than a trustless one, and the design's answers are that you choose the mint, the app ships with none, balances are never pooled across mints, and consolidation lets you leave one.
