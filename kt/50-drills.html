Glossary

Cryptography

AEADAuthenticated encryption with associated data. Encrypts and authenticates in one operation, plus extra bytes authenticated but not encrypted.
ChaCha20-Poly1305The AEAD used throughout. Software cipher with no table lookups, so it is naturally constant time.
XChaCha20-Poly1305Same, with a 24-byte nonce, so random nonces are safe. Used for private channels and Nostr DMs.
Curve25519The elliptic curve. X25519 is its key agreement form, Ed25519 its signature form.
DLEQDiscrete log equivalence. A zero-knowledge proof that a Cashu mint signed a proof, verifiable offline. Proves origin, not freshness.
Double RatchetSignal's algorithm. A symmetric ratchet per message plus a DH ratchet per reply, giving per-message forward secrecy and post-compromise security.
Exporter secretA third HKDF output of the Noise split, descending from the chaining key. What Airhop seeds the ratchet from.
Forward secrecyA key stolen tomorrow does not reveal messages sent today.
Handshake hash (h)A running digest of every byte that went over the wire. Public. Safe as a channel binder, never as key material.
HKDFExtract then expand. Turns non-uniform input into any number of independent keys.
HMACKeyed hash. Not vulnerable to length extension, unlike a bare hash of key plus message.
Noise XXThree-message mutually authenticating handshake with forward secrecy and identity hiding. Airhop's session protocol.
Noise XOne-way sealing to a known static key. No forward secrecy. Used for courier envelopes.
Post-compromise securityThe conversation becomes secure again after a compromise, once fresh entropy is injected. Needs a reply.
PrologueBytes mixed into the Noise handshake hash at initialisation. Empty here, but the mix step still runs, and skipping it broke interoperability.
Safety numberSix words over both parties' keys, read aloud to verify.
secp256k1Bitcoin's curve. Used for Nostr identity, geohash identities and Cashu.
TOFUTrust on first use. The first key seen for a peer stands, and no announce may replace it.
X3DHSignal's asynchronous key agreement using published prekeys. Deliberately not used: Noise seeds the ratchet, and prekeys are gossiped rather than published to a server.

Radio and transport

ATTAttribute Protocol. The layer under GATT. Its 512-byte maximum attribute value is Airhop's frame budget.
BLEBluetooth Low Energy. Not Bluetooth Classic. No pairing required, which is what makes a stranger mesh possible.
dBmPower relative to a milliwatt, logarithmic. +3 dB doubles, -6 dB halves distance in free space.
Duty cycleFraction of time a radio transmits or scans. A battery lever on BLE, a legal limit on LoRa.
Fresnel zoneThe ellipsoid around a radio path that needs clearing. Why height beats antenna gain.
GATTThe service and characteristic model over ATT. Airhop uses one service and one characteristic as a byte pipe.
LoRaChirp spread spectrum on sub-GHz. Kilometres of range for kilobits. No phone has the radio.
mDNSMulticast DNS, local service discovery. The designed and unshipped LAN transport.
MTUThe negotiated ATT message size. Decides whether a write can be unacknowledged. Distinct from the 512-byte attribute limit.
RSSIReceived signal strength in dBm. Always negative. Not distance.
Spreading factorLoRa's range-versus-airtime dial, SF7 to SF12. Each step doubles airtime.
WiFi AwareAndroid's direct device-to-device WiFi. API 26 to discover, API 29 for the data path. Never cross-platform.

Power, radio exposure and Lightning

The elevator answers

QuestionAnswer
What is it?A messaging app that works with no internet, by passing messages phone to phone over Bluetooth, up to seven hops.
Why?Every mainstream messenger stops when the network does, and networks are shut down, congested and surveilled routinely.
How is it private?Noise XX sessions with a Double Ratchet on top, so the phones relaying carry sealed envelopes they cannot open.
Do I need an account?No. Your identity is a key pair made on the phone. There is no create-account screen anywhere.
Who runs the servers?Nobody. There are none. The internet layer uses hundreds of independent Nostr relays that other people run.
How far does it reach?210 to 350 metres, if there are people with the app standing in between. Density is the limit, not distance.
Can it send money?Yes, Cashu ecash, offline, phone to phone. Internet is needed only to top up or cash out.
What is the catch?Other people need it installed. That is the honest weakness, and it is why the wire protocol is shared with bitchat.

For a sceptic

  • "Nobody else has this installed, so it is useless." Correct, and it is the real limitation. Two mitigations exist. Airhop shares bitchat's wire protocol byte for byte, so both user bases form one mesh with no configuration. And relay hardware puts a node where nobody stands. Neither makes an empty field work. The product is designed to be installed before it is needed, which makes distribution the hard problem rather than engineering.
  • "Bluetooth mesh apps have been tried and failed." Bridgefy is the usual example, and it failed on security rather than on the mesh: it was found to be unencrypted and trivially impersonable during exactly the protests it was promoted for. That is an argument for getting the cryptography right, which is why every packet here is signed, sessions are Noise XX, and the threat model assumes an attacker in the room. Briar has run the same architecture successfully for years.
  • "Why not just use Signal?" Use Signal. It is better at what it does, and this is not competing with it. Signal ties an account to a phone number and stops entirely when the network does. Airhop is aimed at the moment after that, when there is no network to be excellent on.
  • "Seven hops of 30 metres is nothing." It is a venue, a city block, a march, a campus, which is exactly the scale the product targets. And it is not the whole story: the internet layer covers distance when a connection exists, and the courier covers time when it does not, by having a person physically carry a sealed message until they meet the recipient.
  • "This is a hackathon project." It started as one. What it is now is 200,000 lines with a multi-device simulator running fully isolated copies of the app on a modelled radio, conformance tests that parse another project's source and fail on drift, and a documented list of its own flaws including the ones it cannot fix. The right criticism is the absence of an external audit, which the project states in its own README and has scheduled.

For a security engineer

  • State the threat model. Anyone in radio range can transmit anything: forge every plaintext header field, replay captured packets, mint unlimited internally-consistent identities, and drop or alter anything passing through them. They cannot break Ed25519, X25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305 or SHA-256 preimage resistance. Out of scope and stated: physical proximity, a stable peer ID, courier tag linkability, who is talking to whom on the mesh, the fact that Tor is in use, traffic timing correlation, a compromised OS, and mint trust.
  • Why does a valid signature not stop impersonation? Because an announce is signed with a key inside that same announce, so it proves internal consistency only. The victim's Noise public key is broadcast in the clear, so an attacker copies the peer ID and Noise key, attaches their own signing key, and signs. Both checks pass. Only refusing to replace a key already pinned to that peer ID stops it, with the single exception of a key proven inside a completed Noise session, which requires the private half.
  • What was the worst bug found, and why did it matter? The Double Ratchet root key was seeded from the Noise handshake hash. mixHash absorbs only bytes that went over the wire, so that value is public, and handshakes flood the mesh at TTL 7. Any bystander could capture the three packets, recompute the root key, and forge or decrypt every ratcheted message in that conversation. Fixed by seeding from the exporter secret, a third HKDF output of the same split, which descends from the chaining key and therefore from ephemeral private keys destroyed at split.
  • How is Sybil handled? Bounded damage rather than prevention, and the design says so. Caps with eviction that protects physical facts: registry and radar at 200 with oldest-first eviction that never drops a peer holding a real link, and direct standing following the link lifecycle rather than a forgeable TTL field. There is no proof of work and no cost to identity creation, so an attacker can still fill the non-direct slots. That is the available answer in a permissionless system.
  • Where does the design fall short of its own claims? Prekey bundles are not reconciled through gossip sync, so courier envelopes silently fall back to static-key sealing and lose the forward secrecy the README advertises. Nostr DMs have no forward secrecy at all. Private channel keys never rotate and cannot be revoked. There is no key rotation or revocation anywhere. And there is no external audit yet.

For a backend or devops engineer

  • How does it scale? Not the way a server does, because there is no capacity to add. The scarce resource is airtime in a fixed, shared 2.4 GHz band. More users improves reach and increases contention simultaneously. The real question is at what density delivery probability drops below useful, and the levers are transmissions per message and hop depth. 25 phones converge in the simulation; 200 is untested, and the documented mitigation, fanout subsetting, is not implemented.
  • What is your amplification story? TTL bounds depth. Dedup bounds repetition. Degree-scaled jitter prevents the broadcast storm. Sync requests and every packet answering one ride at TTL 0 so they stop at the requester, responses are capped at 8 per peer per 30 seconds, and the rate limit is checked before the expensive diff pass. Gateway uplinks are capped at 10 per depositor per minute with the depositor's signature verified. Courier deposits are capped by pool, tier and depositor. Decompression is bounded at the declared size while inflating, not after.
  • How do you observe a system with no telemetry? You largely do not, and that is a deliberate cost of sending nothing anywhere. The substitute is a simulation you can re-run: every scenario prints a seed, so a failure is reproducible byte for byte, and the invariants are properties that hold under any interleaving rather than scripted expectations. The gap worth closing is local diagnostics a field tester can read and report themselves.
  • What is your retry and backoff strategy? Layered. Radio controllers are reconcilers: a desired state, an observed outcome, one idempotent pass, and a backoff ladder, with "unsupported" latched forever because that is not a state a device leaves. Fragment writes back off 60 ms on refusal and give up after 250 consecutive. The outbox retries event-driven with a 10 second floor and a 45 second sweep, capped at 8 attempts over 7 days. A refused write is explicitly not a dead link, because the stack says the same thing when its queue is merely full.
  • What breaks in a partition? Nothing, structurally. A partition is the normal case. Messages queue in the outbox, courier envelopes are handed to people who might walk across the gap, and gossip sync reconciles the history when the partition heals. If one side has internet, the mesh bridge can stitch two islands over a geohash rendezvous cell.

For a mobile engineer

  • Why one TypeScript codebase rather than two native ones? Because bitchat's two codebases drifted until an Android fragment size mismatch broke cross-platform messaging for months with nobody noticing. One implementation means a protocol bug exists on both platforms or neither, and a fix lands on both at the same instant. The cost is a JavaScript bridge in the path, which is not the bottleneck: the radio moves 18 KiB/s.
  • How do you keep the mesh alive in the background? iOS: bluetooth-central and bluetooth-peripheral background modes, plus CoreBluetooth state restoration, which relaunches the app on a BLE event and is why the keychain item must be AFTER_FIRST_UNLOCK. Android: a foreground service typed connectedDevice, plus neverForLocation on BLUETOOTH_SCAN, without which the service ran and no scan results were delivered at all.
  • What is the most surprising platform behaviour? A backgrounded iPhone is invisible to an Android scanner. CoreBluetooth moves the service UUID into the advertisement overflow area and drops the local name, so only another iOS device can discover it. Established links keep working, so the symptom is specifically "cannot find" rather than "cannot message". Not fixable in app code, and modelled explicitly in the simulation's radio fabric so it cannot be forgotten.
  • How is battery handled? Policy in TypeScript as a pure function of four facts, mechanism in Kotlin. Four modes moving five knobs together, because a duty-cycled low-power scan beside a full-power advertise saves almost nothing. Backgrounded gives power saver, charging in the foreground gives performance, otherwise the battery band decides, and foreground on battery is balanced rather than performance. Hysteresis of +3 percent to climb out of a band, because bitchat re-resolves per 1 percent and every flip restarts the scanner. iOS is a declared no-op, because CoreBluetooth exposes no scan-rate control.
  • What must a new contributor never do? Run npx expo prebuild, which regenerates the native trees and deletes every module. Rename a keychain item, an HKDF separator or an MMKV partition id, which are addresses rather than labels. Change a shared wire constant. And treat a refused GATT write as a dead link.
  • When does Airhop ask for each permission, and why in that order? Bluetooth first, before the mesh starts, because nothing works without it and on Android a second refusal makes it permanently blocked. A one-time primer sheet precedes it, explaining the asks. Location next, folded into the Bluetooth batch on Android 11 and below where BLE scanning genuinely needs it, and asked separately on iOS after the mesh is already running. Notifications last, because its consequence is the mildest. Camera, photos and microphone are asked on demand, at the moment the feature needs them, which is the only time no explanation is required. Every ask outside the batched Bluetooth run is awaited one at a time: concurrent prompts on a fresh install swallowed the notification prompt and sometimes crashed.
  • The BLE permission prompt never returns. What happens to the app? Nothing bad. Every await before the mesh is created is time-boxed, and this one at 60 seconds. Past the deadline nothing is published about the permission, because "the OS never answered" is not one of the answers it could have given, and guessing would put a red banner over a permission the user may have granted. The mesh starts anyway, and the radio controller reads the real grant off the device on its first reconcile. The prompt really can be orphaned: it is hosted by another process, and an OEM that reaps the Activity behind a foreground service destroys it mid-dialog.
  • Someone pastes you a contact code. What can you do with it, and what can it not do? You can encrypt to that identity and open a DM route immediately, because the card carries the Noise key, the signing key and the Nostr key, and its peer ID is checked against SHA-256 of its own Noise key before anything is stored. What it cannot do is overwrite keys already bound to that peer ID, because nothing about a pasted card proves who sent it. It is stored as source: "link" with no verification. Only a camera scan of the other person's live screen earns inPerson, and only that may re-pin. The gap is closed later by comparing a six word safety number over a channel you already trust, which imports no keys and therefore needs no such gate.

For a cryptographer

  • Walk me through Noise XX. Message one: the initiator sends a fresh ephemeral public key in the clear. Message two: the responder sends its ephemeral, mixes ee which creates the first cipher key, then sends its static key encrypted under it, then mixes es. Message three: the initiator sends its static key encrypted and mixes se. Three DH operations: ee gives forward secrecy, es authenticates the responder, se authenticates the initiator. The static keys are transmitted after the first DH, which is what gives identity hiding.
  • Why not use the transcript hash as the ratchet seed? Because mixHash absorbs only bytes that went over the wire, so it is public to anyone who captured the handshake. The exporter secret descends from the chaining key, which absorbed ephemeral DH outputs whose private halves are destroyed at split, so it cannot be reconstructed from long-term keys or recomputed by an observer. It is free because HKDF chains: asking for a third output leaves the first two bit-identical, so bitchat's transport keys are unchanged.
  • Justify a zero nonce in the Double Ratchet. The rule is never to reuse a (key, nonce) pair. The ratchet derives a fresh 32-byte message key per message from the chain, so each key is used exactly once, and with a single-use key there is no second message under it for the nonce to collide with. Any fixed nonce is safe. The dangerous case is a fixed nonce with a reused key, which the Noise transport avoids with a counter and which XChaCha20 avoids with a 24-byte random nonce.
  • Where is forward secrecy absent? Nostr DMs, entirely: a static ECDH between two long-lived secp256k1 identities, so compromising either key opens every archived gift wrap. Courier envelopes sealed to a static key when no prekey bundle was available, which happens silently because prekeys are not reconciled through gossip sync. Private channels, which use one symmetric key that never rotates. Group messages within an epoch. And attachments, which never get ratchet treatment.
  • Why not MLS for groups? MLS needs a delivery service giving every member the same ordered sequence of commits, and a flooding mesh with partitions cannot provide one: two members in separate partitions would advance to incompatible epochs with no reconciliation path. Its logarithmic advantage also only appears at hundreds of members, where Airhop caps at 16. The honest counterpoint is that Airhop's groups have no per-message forward secrecy within an epoch, which is genuinely weaker.

For a designer

  • How does this app decide what a spacing, a radius or a duration should be? It does not decide, it looks it up. Nine spacings on a 4pt grid, nine type sizes, seven radii, three durations, one accent. The rule is that a value is right when a rule produced it and wrong when somebody picked it, and the corollary is that solving something once is only half the job: it is finished when the answer is a token, a shared component or a lint rule. Every inconsistency that ever shipped in this codebase was a correct decision that never propagated.
  • Name three details users will never consciously notice and explain why each was worth the effort. Concentric radius, where inner = outer - padding is the only value at which a nested box holds a constant corner gap; below it the corners pinch and above it they bulge, and the eye reads two shapes fighting rather than one nesting. Letter-spacing applied after the last glyph, which makes a tracked string measure wider than it draws so centring leaves it half a track short. And includeFontPadding: false, an Android default that reserves ascender room a digit never uses, so a numeral centred in a circle sits correctly on iOS and low on Android. None of the three shows in a screenshot and all three show in the hand, which is the whole argument for them.
  • Which apps does this borrow from, and where does it deliberately not? Delivery ticks, message-info sheets and document subtitles from WhatsApp; the inverted outgoing bubble from iMessage; unified cross-thread search from the WhatsApp, Telegram and Signal convention; the activity tab from Instagram; safety numbers and keep-anyone-you-message from Signal; the 44pt floor, separator inset and sheet drag behaviour from Apple HIG and Material 3 where the two agree. That is Jakob's Law applied deliberately. The divergence is colour: every one of those apps uses a brand hue as its accent and this one uses near-black, because it has four network states, two trust levels and three delivery states that all genuinely need distinguishing, and a brand colour competing with them would make the meaningful ones harder to read.

For a product person

  • Who is this for? People whose network is unavailable, surveilled or shut down: national shutdowns, disaster zones, mass gatherings where cells are congested, and places with no coverage. It is installed before it is needed, which is why the languages shipped are the ones spoken where shutdowns happen: Persian, Arabic, Urdu, Hindi, Bengali, Punjabi, Tamil, Indonesian, Filipino, Nepali, Ukrainian and Russian.
  • What is the hardest UX problem? Telling the truth about uncertainty. A message can leave the radio and reach nobody. A peer can be visible and unreachable. Money can be committed and undeliverable. The rule is that an honest uncertain state beats a confident wrong one, and it is enforced in type signatures rather than copy: send results carry link counts, the wipe reports whether the keys actually went, offline ecash is stored as unverified.
  • Why does the app open on the Mesh tab? Because the first question a user of this app has is whether it is working and whether anyone is nearby. In a normal messenger connectivity is assumed, so opening to conversations answers the right question. Here connectivity is the variable.
  • Why is there no account recovery? Because recovery requires a party who can restore your identity, and that party is exactly what the product refuses to have. The identity is a key on one phone, stored THIS_DEVICE_ONLY so it is excluded even from encrypted backups. Losing the phone means losing the identity. The five-second key generation screen exists specifically so the user learns this at the start rather than discovering it.

The five hardest questions

  • "Your README says four things ship that do not." That is correct, and it is the top finding in chapter 49. iOS MultipeerConnectivity, the local AI assistant, and the AT Protocol and ActivityPub bridges are all presented as features and are roadmap items or removed code. The comparison table also lists desktop, web and CLI platforms that do not exist. The fix is a status column, not a rewrite. It matters because a reviewer who finds one overstatement discounts the security claims too, and those are sound.
  • "Prove your crypto is right." I cannot, and neither can the project, and it says so. What exists: audited primitive libraries, constructions that follow published specifications, byte-level test vectors, a conformance harness that parses another implementation's source, and two critical findings already caught and fixed. What does not exist: an external audit, which is scheduled for v1.9.0. Anyone treating "not obviously wrong to a careful reviewer" as equivalent to "audited" is making a mistake the project has explicitly warned against.
  • "What happens at 500 users in one square?" Unknown, honestly. 25 phones converge in the simulation and a seeded soak holds every invariant across eight. Above the low tens is untested. The reason it worries me specifically is that Airhop relays to every non-ingress link where bitchat relays to a message-ID-seeded subset, and Airhop's own protocol document specifies the subset. The repair mechanism that makes subsetting safe, gossip sync, is already running. So the mitigation is known, specified, and not implemented.
  • "Is courier mail really anonymous?" No, and the project says so first. The carrier cannot read the content and cannot tell who sent it. But the routing tag is an HMAC keyed on the recipient's public Noise key, which every announce broadcasts, so anyone who has heard one announce can compute that peer's tags for any day and follow their mail. It is inherited from bitchat, which documents the same flaw in its own implementation and notes that its whitepaper's unlinkability claim does not hold. Fixing it needs a v2 tag keyed on a shared secret, agreed across implementations.
  • "Why should anyone trust a solo project with this?" They should not trust it more than the evidence supports, and the README says exactly that: not externally security-reviewed, do not rely on it for sensitive use cases. What the evidence does support is that the protocol is correct against a vendored reference, that it is tested with a multi-device simulator most funded teams do not build, and that the project documents its own flaws before anyone else finds them. Bus factor of one is the real risk, and it is a reason to contribute rather than a reason to dismiss.

The last thing

If you remember one sentence from this manual, make it this one: never claim more than you know. It explains the delivery states, the wipe result, the DLEQ result, the Tor banner, the radar rings, the unverified ecash bucket, and the honest list of what is out of scope. It is also the standard the documentation should be held to, which is the substance of chapter 49.
