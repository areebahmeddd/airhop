Threat model

The attacker is assumed to be in the room, holding a radio, able to transmit anything. This chapter enumerates what they can do, what stops them, and the six things that stop nothing at all.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Anyone standing near you can shout anything into the air. They can pretend to be someone else, shout the same thing a thousand times, or record what you said and shout it again tomorrow. The app assumes all of that is happening. It checks the handwriting on every note, refuses to change its address book once it has written someone down, ignores notes that are too old, and puts a hard limit on how much of your phone anyone else can use.

The attacker

They CAN

  • Transmit anything, from anywhere in radio range.
  • Forge any plaintext header field: senderID, ttl, flags, timestamp.
  • Replay captured packets.
  • Mint unlimited identities, each internally consistent.
  • Drop, delay or alter anything passing through them as a relay.
  • Run a Nostr relay, or observe one.
  • Observe BLE advertisements passively.
  • Seize a device.

They CANNOT

  • Break Ed25519, X25519 or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
  • Find a SHA-256 preimage.
  • Complete a Noise XX handshake as somebody else.
  • Produce a signature for a key they do not hold.
  • Read a Noise session they are not a party to.

That is a realistic model for a crowd, a protest, a conference or a disaster zone, and it is deliberately strong: it grants the attacker everything the physics allows and nothing the mathematics forbids.

Forgery and impersonation

AttackCountermeasureTest
Message forgery. Post as somebody else. Ed25519 signature verified against the key bound to the claimed sender. A missing key or a missing SIGNED flag is a failed check, never a skipped one. C01, C02
Identity impersonation. Claim someone else's peer ID. peerID == SHA-256(noiseStaticPubKey)[0:16], enforced on announces and on completed Noise sessions. Preimage resistance means a key cannot be constructed to hash to a chosen ID. C08
Signing-key substitution. Copy the victim's public Noise key off the air, attach your own signing key, self-sign. Two tiers. An announce is self-signed, so TOFU pinning holds the first key seen and never replaces it over the air. A key proven inside a Noise session (payload 0x21) outranks that and may correct a pin an attacker won the race for. No announce can overwrite a proven key, and only an in-person QR scan may re-pin otherwise. C08
Capability downgrade. Announce bit 8 off for a victim to force attachments into the clear. Announced bits are a discovery hint and never authorise a change in how we send. Encrypted private media is selected only on an authenticated capability. M09
Man in the middle on a session. Noise XX mutual authentication, with the authenticated static key required to derive the peer ID it claims. A06
Forged departure. Send a LEAVE carrying a victim's ID to evict them from everyone's view. Checked against the pinned signing key before the relay decision, so an unverifiable one is neither acted on nor passed to nodes that may check less strictly. C09
Malicious relay injecting messages. A relay cannot produce the sender's signature, and forwarding is separate from delivery. R05
Confused-deputy delivery. Make a relay render somebody else's private content. Directed packets are relayed but rendered only by the addressee. M08
Group takeover. Craft a state naming yourself creator at a higher epoch. A group pins its creatorFingerprint and refuses a state naming a different creator even at a higher epoch. Enforced in two places, because a group state can either update or delete a group: the store pins it for every write, and groupStateAction pins it before the removal branch, which never reaches the store. F01
Group eviction by a stranger. A group ID travels in the clear on every group message so relays can carry it. Without the ordering above, anyone who saw one could craft a self-signed state naming themselves creator with a roster omitting the victim, and the victim's client would destroy its own key and drop the room. The creator pin is checked first. This is a good example of a check whose order is the security property. F01

Not everything is signed, and that is deliberate

Noise handshake messages are unsigned, matching bitchat, because the peer may not hold our signing key yet and the handshake authenticates itself. Messages inside a Noise session or a ratchet carry no redundant signature, since the session already authenticates them. Fragments are unsigned because the inner packet is signed and re-verified after reassembly. Signatures are required exactly where a claimed sender is otherwise unverifiable: announces, public and private channel messages, attachments, voice frames, board posts and gateway uplinks.

Sybil

The hardest structural problem in any permissionless network. Anyone can mint an unlimited number of identities, each with a real key pair, a correctly derived peer ID, and a valid self-signature. Nothing about any individual one is detectably wrong, which is what makes it a resource problem rather than a signature problem.

VectorBound
Peer registry growthCapped at 200, oldest-first eviction that never drops a peer holding a real BLE link.
Radar growthSame cap, same rule.
Prekey bundle storeCapped at 200 peers.
Fake "direct" peersA link binds to the first peer that announces directly on it. A later claim from a different peer ID on that link is downgraded to relayed rather than dropped.
Courier pool40 envelopes, 20 reserved for the verified tier, 2 per unknown depositor.
Sync amplification8 responses per peer per 30 s, attributed by link rather than by claimed sender ID.
Gateway uplinks10 per depositor per minute, with the depositor's signature checked.
PropagationTTL 7 bounds how far any of it travels.

The vector that made every other cap useless

A peer counted as "directly connected" whenever packet.ttl == 7, a field an attacker sets freely. Direct peers are the ones protected from eviction, so one hostile radio over one real link could announce hundreds of invented identities that all looked directly connected and were all immune to trimming. Five hundred invented peers survived a flood the caps were specifically there to bound. Each one also overwrote the link's peer binding (today peerByLink) for that link, breaking RSSI attribution and disconnect handling for the genuine peer on it.

The fix pairs the TTL heuristic with a physical fact: a link binds to one peer, and direct standing follows the link lifecycle, which no amount of announcing can fake. bitchat rejects the same case by name as directSenderMismatch. Scenario C04.

The honest limitation: Sybil resistance here is bounded damage, not prevention. There is no proof of work, no stake, no identity cost. An attacker can still fill 200 registry slots with invented peers, and the mitigation is that direct neighbours are protected and everything else ages out. In a permissionless system with no cost to identity creation, that is the available answer.

Replay

A captured packet is correctly signed forever. Signatures alone stop forgery, not replay.

LayerMechanismWindow
Freshness at ingressEvery packet is held to the timestamp window and is neither relayed nor acted on outside it. Relaying a stale packet costs everyone downstream airtime and re-seeds an attacker's recording into a mesh that had already forgotten it.+/- 2 minutes
AnnouncesA tighter, symmetric window. The union of what both bitchat clients accept: iOS uses 900 s backwards only, Android 600 s symmetric.15 minutes, symmetric
Live voiceStale audio is worthless, and replaying a recorded burst at somebody later is a real attack.30 seconds

Noise transportA 1024-nonce sliding window, the same construction IPsec and WireGuard use.1024 messages of reordering
Double RatchetMessage keys are single use and deleted.n/a
Nostr rumorsThe outer timestamp is randomised by design, so the inner one is the only claim about send time and nothing signs it into a window. Bounded by the subscription lookback plus skew.Lookback + 15 min

Why dedup cannot be the replay bound

Its window is five minutes and its state is per device, so it says nothing about a phone that never heard the original. Freshness is the part that travels. The two do different jobs and both are needed.

The one exemption is gossip sync, which exists to replay old packets. A response carries IS_RSR and ttl = 0, and the receiver skips the window only when the packet claims RSR and arrives from a peer it has an outstanding request to, inside a 30 second response window. The flag alone is a sender's claim; the pending request is the receiver's own record. That is precisely why requests are unicast: a broadcast request has no peer to register against.

And a nice detail: if two or more different peers send packets outside the window, the app infers that its own clock is wrong rather than blaming them, and shows a banner. One peer sending stale packets is that peer replaying; several peers disagreeing at once points at what they have in common.

Denial of service and amplification

This is the section a backend engineer will interrogate hardest, because a flooding mesh is an amplifier by construction.

AttackAmplification without a controlControl
Packet flood Unbounded. Every node relays to every neighbour, forever, around cycles. TTL 7 bounds depth. Dedup bounds repetition. Jitter (10 to 220 ms, widening with degree) prevents the broadcast storm and lets the first copy silence the rest.
Sync amplification One small request draws back the responder's entire store, and if responses were relayed they would re-flood the whole mesh. REQUEST_SYNC and every packet answering one ride at ttl 0, so they stop at the requester. Responses capped at 8 per peer per 30 s. The rate limit is checked before decoding, because the diff pass is the expensive part. Attribution is by physical link, so one peer minting sender IDs does not get unbounded budgets.
Courier flood Fill every nearby phone's storage with mail. 40 envelope pool, 2 per unknown depositor, 5 per contact, 20-slot verified sub-cap, 16 KiB per envelope, 24-hour TTL, expiry beyond TTL plus 1 hour slack refused rather than clamped.
Spray budget inflation copies is unauthenticated input deciding how often a carrier re-emits. An unclamped byte makes every carrier an amplifier for whoever claims 255. Clamped to 8 on decode, and the budget only rises before the first spray.
Gateway abuse Make somebody else's phone publish arbitrary events to relays under their IP. Kind 20000 only, 15 minute freshness, the cell must match one this gateway serves, the depositor's signature is checked, 10 uplinks per depositor per minute, 30 downlinks per minute total, queues bounded at 20 total and 5 per depositor.
Decompression bomb 25 bytes on the wire, gigabytes after inflation. An out-of-memory crash any unauthenticated peer in range can trigger. Output capped at the declared size while inflating, refused at the first byte over. A valid stream inflates to exactly the declared size, so nothing legitimate is affected.
Ratchet skip flood Claim N = 4,000,000,000 and force four billion HMAC operations from one packet. MAX_SKIP of 1000, and a skipped-key cache capped at 1000 entries.
Fragment table exhaustion Open unlimited assemblies. 128 concurrent, oldest evicted, 30 second idle timeout, per-stream byte ceiling, and the size check runs before the table is touched so a doomed fragment is not an eviction primitive.
Pong amplification Ping flood producing a pong flood. Minimum 100 ms spacing between pong replies on one ingress link.
Announce storm Twelve phones connecting at once put roughly 9,200 announce and 6,600 prekey packets on the air in half a second, because each link-up minted a freshly timestamped packet no relay could dedup. Both are reused within a window so relays can deduplicate them, and the bundle goes only to the new link.
Nostr relay flood Overwhelm the client with events. The inbound pump yields the thread between batches. Reads are bounded at 6 seconds. Publishes are bounded at 8.

The one amplification control that is documented and absent

PROTOCOLS.md lists a fanout subset of roughly ceil(sqrt(n)) links as a routing constant. There is no such code: relayPacket writes to every link except the ingress one. Full fanout means the number of transmissions per message grows with the number of edges in the graph, and airtime is the scarce resource. Tested and converging at 25 phones; untested at 200. bitchat's message-ID-seeded subset is the available mitigation and it is not taken. Chapter 45 does the arithmetic.

Resource exhaustion

Every unbounded collection reachable from an unauthenticated packet is a vulnerability. The audit shape is: find every Map, Set or array written to from the receive path, and check it has a cap.

Two of these were genuine findings. The peer registry and radar grew without bound for the life of the process: reads were TTL-bounded so a flood never made a fake peer look reachable, but the underlying maps were never swept, so the memory was held forever and every reachablePeers() scan walked all of it. And the courier store accepted any expiry, so one envelope stamped years out held a pool slot for as long as the app was installed.

Explicitly out of scope

Naming these is the most valuable part of the threat model, because an unnamed gap is a gap somebody assumes is covered.

  1. Physical proximity. BLE mesh reveals that you are near certain peers.
  2. A location you chose to send. A location pin carries real coordinates. It is encrypted inside the recipient's Noise session, so no relay reads it, but the recipient holds it and can do what they like with it. Sent only on an explicit tap, to one contact, never automatically, never as a reply in kind, never forwarded onward by the app, and never couriered or gossiped.
  3. A stable peer ID. It does not rotate.
  4. Attachment confidentiality in a public room. A photo in #bluetooth is signed but not encrypted, exactly like the text beside it.
  5. The fact that Tor is in use. No bridges or pluggable transports on either platform.
  6. Traffic timing correlation. An observer watching several BLE radios could infer communication patterns.
  7. Courier mail linkability. Inherited from bitchat, documented in both projects.
  8. Who you are talking to on the mesh. Headers carry sender and recipient IDs in the clear.
  9. Public message authorship, some of the time. The origin TTL range weakens the marker rather than removing it.
  10. A compromised OS. All guarantees are void.
  11. Mint trust. Cashu requires trusting the mint to honour redemption.

Beyond what the project already documents, these are the items I would put in front of an auditor. They are argued in full in chapter 49.

Drills

  • State the attacker model in two sentences. Anyone within radio range can transmit anything: they can forge every plaintext header field, replay captured packets, mint unlimited internally-consistent identities, and drop or alter anything passing through them as a relay. They cannot break Ed25519, X25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305, or SHA-256 preimage resistance.
  • Why does a valid signature not stop impersonation on its own? Because an announce is signed with a key carried inside that same announce, so the signature proves only internal consistency. The victim's public Noise key is broadcast in the clear, so an attacker copies the peer ID and Noise key, substitutes their own signing key, and signs. Both the derivation check and the signature check pass. What stops it is refusing to replace a key already bound to that peer ID, with the sole exception of a key proven inside a completed Noise session, which requires possession of the private half.
  • A security engineer asks how you bound sync amplification. Answer precisely. Four controls. Requests and every packet answering one ride at TTL 0, so a rejoining peer's catch-up stops at the requester rather than re-flooding the mesh. Responses are capped at 8 per peer per 30 seconds. The rate limit is checked before the filter is decoded, because the diff pass is the expensive part and a looping peer should not make us pay for it. And the budget is attributed to the peer bound to the physical link rather than to the claimed sender ID, so one peer minting IDs cannot get unbounded budgets over one radio link.
  • How is a Sybil flood bounded, and what is the honest limitation? Caps with eviction that protects physical facts: the registry and radar are capped at 200 with oldest-first eviction that never drops a peer holding a real BLE link, and direct standing follows the link lifecycle rather than a forgeable TTL field. The honest limitation is that this is bounded damage, not prevention. There is no proof of work, no stake, and no cost to creating an identity, so an attacker can still fill the non-direct slots. In a permissionless system that is the available answer, and the design says so rather than claiming Sybil resistance.
  • Why is a two minute freshness window enough, and what backs it up? Phones are NTP synced and an offline real-time clock drifts by seconds a day rather than minutes, so the failure is rare and visible: a peer whose clock is wrong cannot talk to anyone, and the app detects that case by noticing that several different peers disagree at once. It is backed up by the deduplicator for the local repeat case, by a tighter 15 minute symmetric window on announces, and by a 30 second window on live voice where staleness is itself the attack.
  • Which single out-of-scope item would you most want fixed? Peer ID rotation, because it is the root of several others. A non-rotating ID makes a device linkable across every session, and the courier tag's linkability is a direct consequence of the same long-lived key being public. It is also genuinely hard: rotation breaks the direct-link rule, the courier tags, contact pinning and the outbox at once, which is why bitchat has an entire design document on it and has not shipped it either.
