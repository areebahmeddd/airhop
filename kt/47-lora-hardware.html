In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Passing a message phone to phone only works if there is a person standing every thirty metres. Across an empty car park there is nobody, so the chain breaks. A relay is a small box with a battery and a radio that you stick on a wall. It does not talk, it just listens and repeats, so it is the person standing in the gap. Some of these boxes also have a second, much slower radio that reaches kilometres, so two boxes far apart can pass messages to each other when no phone could.

The problem hardware solves

  WITHOUT A RELAY

    TTL 7 is irrelevant. There is no hop 4. Two islands, no path.

  WITH ONE RELAY

    └────────────┘            └──────────────┘               └────────────┘

    One box, mains or solar, and the two islands are one mesh.

  WITH A LoRa TRUNK

    ┌───────┐                                              ┌───────┐
    │ ▣ BLE │◀════════ 915 MHz LoRa, 2 to 10 km ══════════▶│ ▣ BLE │

     phones                                                 phones

    Two neighbourhoods joined with no internet and no phone in between.

This is the answer to the honest criticism of any phone mesh: reach scales with user density, and in a sparse area there is no mesh at all. Hardware converts "we need more users" into "we need one box".

Sub-GHz radio

Chapter 07 established the link budget. Sub-GHz changes two of its terms dramatically. 

  FREE SPACE PATH LOSS = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) - 147.55

  The frequency term is what changes:

     2400 MHz  ->  20 log10(2.4e9)  = 187.6
      915 MHz  ->  20 log10(915e6)  = 179.2
      868 MHz  ->  20 log10(868e6)  = 178.8
      433 MHz  ->  20 log10(433e6)  = 172.7

  915 MHz is ~8.4 dB better than 2.4 GHz at the same distance.
  8.4 dB is roughly 2.6x the range for the same power.
  433 MHz is ~15 dB better, roughly 5.6x.

The trade is stark: kilometres of range for kilobits of bandwidth. That is the right trade for a backbone between fixed relays carrying text, and completely wrong for anything a phone does.

The band is not a preference, it is a legal fact of where the hardware is. A node bought for one region and used in another can be illegal to operate.

LoRa and chirp spread spectrum

LoRa is a proprietary physical layer from Semtech. Its trick is chirp spread spectrum, and it is genuinely elegant. 

  ORDINARY MODULATION
     encode a bit by making the signal louder, or shifting frequency
     a little. To read it, you need the signal ABOVE the noise floor.

  CHIRP SPREAD SPECTRUM
     encode a symbol as a CHIRP: a tone sweeping from one frequency to
     another over a fixed time. The starting point of the sweep is the
     data.

        freq                        freq
         ▲   ╱                       ▲       ╱
         │  ╱                        │      ╱
         │ ╱                         │   ╱
         │╱                          │  ╱
         └────────▶ time             └────────▶ time
          symbol A                    symbol B
        (starts low)              (starts mid, wraps)

     The receiver correlates against a known reference chirp. Because
     the energy is spread across the whole bandwidth and across time,
     correlation gain lets it recover a signal
     BELOW THE NOISE FLOOR: down to about -20 dB SNR.

Why "below the noise floor" is not magic

Processing gain. Spreading one symbol over many chip periods means the correlator integrates the signal coherently and the noise incoherently, so the signal-to-noise ratio improves by the spreading factor. You pay for it in time: a symbol takes far longer to transmit, which is exactly why LoRa is slow. Sensitivity and data rate are the same dial.

ParameterRangeEffect
Spreading factor (SF)SF7 to SF12Each step doubles airtime, adds ~2.5 dB sensitivity, and roughly doubles range in open terrain.
Bandwidth125, 250, 500 kHzWider is faster and less sensitive.
Coding rate4/5 to 4/8Forward error correction. More redundancy, more airtime.
TX powerUp to +20 dBm typicallyRegion-capped.
Sensitivity-123 dBm at SF7 to -137 dBm at SF12Compare BLE at about -90 to -95 dBm. That is 40 dB better, which is a factor of 10,000 in power.

Spreading factor, airtime and the law

This is where deployments go wrong, so it is worth the arithmetic.

SFBitrate at 125 kHzAirtime for a 50 byte packetTypical rangePackets/hour at 1% duty cycle
SF7~5.5 kbps~100 ms2 km~360
SF9~1.8 kbps~330 ms5 km~109
SF10~1.0 kbps~700 ms8 km~51
SF12~0.25 kbps~2.5 seconds15 km+~14

The trap

At SF12, a single 50 byte message occupies the channel for two and a half seconds. In Europe, a 1 percent duty cycle means a node may transmit for 36 seconds per hour in total, which is about fourteen messages an hour. In a mesh where every node repeats what it hears, that budget is consumed by forwarding other people's traffic long before your own message gets out.

"Maximum range" settings are therefore usually the wrong choice. The correct spreading factor is the lowest one that reliably closes your specific link, not the highest one the radio offers.

Duty cycle is also why LoRa meshes are fundamentally text and telemetry networks. A 50 KB photo at SF9 is about 4.5 minutes of continuous airtime, which is more than an hour of legal budget in Europe. There is no version of this that carries media.

Meshtastic

The reference open-source LoRa mesh, and the thing most people will compare Airhop's relay story to. It is worth being precise about the difference, because they are complementary rather than competing.

The right framing

They are not alternatives, they solve opposite failure cases. Meshtastic is excellent exactly where a phone mesh has nobody to hop through, and useless in a crowd where everyone would need a device. Airhop is excellent exactly where the people are, and non-existent in an empty valley. A relay node with both radios is the bridge between the two regimes, and that is precisely what Bitle is.

One security difference worth naming: Meshtastic's default channel encryption is a shared symmetric key, so everyone on a channel can read everything and anyone who obtains the key can too. That is a reasonable design for a hiking group and the wrong one for strangers in a protest. Its DM path uses per-key encryption and is stronger.

Antennas for fixed nodes

On a phone the antenna is whatever the manufacturer chose and the app cannot influence it. On a relay it is the single biggest lever, and it is cheap.

AntennaGainPatternUse
PCB trace or chip0 to 2 dBiLumpyWhat ships on a dev board. Adequate indoors, poor outdoors.
Quarter-wave whip~2 dBiDoughnut around the wireThe default upgrade. At 915 MHz it is about 8 cm.
Half-wave dipole2.15 dBiDoughnutThe reference. Good general purpose.
Collinear5 to 8 dBiFlattened doughnutThe right choice for most fixed nodes. Squashes energy toward the horizon, where the other nodes are.
Yagi10 to 15 dBiNarrow beamA dedicated point-to-point trunk between two known sites. Needs aiming.

Three rules that beat any antenna upgrade

  1. Height beats gain. Getting a node two metres higher usually does more than adding 3 dBi, because it clears the Fresnel zone and reduces ground reflection.
  2. Match the polarisation. Two antennas at 90 degrees lose 20 dB or more. Vertical at both ends, by convention.
  3. Never transmit without an antenna connected. The reflected power destroys the transmitter. This is the most common way a first LoRa board dies.

And a note on gain that people get wrong: an antenna is passive. Gain does not amplify, it concentrates. A 12 dBi antenna makes a beam by giving up the directions it no longer covers, which is exactly wrong if your node sits in a valley and the others are above it.

Power and enclosures

USB mainsIndefiniteIndoor, a venue, a shop window.

An outdoor node needs an IP65 enclosure with a gland for the antenna cable, and a note about temperature: LiPo cells do not charge below freezing, and a black box in direct sun can exceed the cell's safe range. Both are common causes of a node that worked for a season and then stopped.

A relay that holds no keys and never announces uses almost no CPU, so the power budget is dominated by radio duty cycle: how often it scans and how often it advertises. That maps directly onto the same trade Airhop's power policy makes on a phone.

How a relay joins an Airhop mesh

The property that makes this possible

Forwarding consults no peer registry and verifies no signature. So a relay needs no standing with anyone: it is handed bytes, reads the header, and puts them back on the air. A bare relay extends an Airhop mesh with no change to this repository and nothing on either phone knowing it is there. 

  THE ENTIRE CONTRACT FOR A BARE REPEATER

    Forward          every unseen packet, on every link but the arrival one
    TTL              decrement before forwarding; drop at ttl <= 1
    Dedup            on packet ID, so a ring forwards each packet once
    Malformed frame  DROP, never fail

  A relay MUST NOT decrypt and MUST NOT reassemble fragments.
  Fragments are forwarded individually; only the addressee reassembles.

Two kinds of relay

A bare repeater

Holds no keys and announces nothing. It stays out of every roster, which costs it nothing, because forwarding never required standing. Invisible to users, which is exactly right for infrastructure.

Cheapest to build, and the weakest thing Airhop has to work with, which is why the tests target it.

A peer relay

Holds a Noise identity and announces like anybody else, which lets it take part in gossip sync and carry courier mail rather than only repeating what it hears.

The cost is that it lands in every phone's peer list looking like a person nobody can message. Which is what the 0xB1 flag below exists to fix.

Announces travel first

A relay does not make two strangers reachable. It makes their announces reachable, and delivery follows. A public message is verified against a signing key learned from an announce, so one sent before that announce has propagated is dropped at the far end. This is correct behaviour and it reads as a delivery bug in the field. Expect it during the first seconds after a relay comes up.

Bitle

Bitle is the most complete third-party relay: an ESP32 node that is a full peer, not a repeater.

CapabilityEffect

The 0xB1 announce TLV

A peer relay announces like a person, so without a marker it sits in every phone's Mesh tab as somebody who never replies. Bitle marks itself with a TLV outside the range either client assigns.

TLVLengthMeaningAirhop
0xB04Firmware version, big endianSkipped
0xB11Flags. Bit 0: dedicated relay. Bit 1: clock authority.Bit 0 is read. Bit-tested rather than compared, so the clock-authority bit and any future bit leave it alone.

Airhop draws those peers as equipment: an aerial in place of the avatar, and an explanation in place of the Message and Send ecash actions.

The rule that keeps this safe

The flag decides presentation and nothing else. It sits inside the signed payload, but a node signs its own announce, so any device can claim to be infrastructure. Since the claim only removes actions from its own row, there is nothing to gain by making it falsely. Bitle states the same contract where it writes the byte.

Never let this flag gate a capability, a trust decision, or a routing choice. Airhop reads it and never writes it, because a phone that claimed to be infrastructure would hide its own Message button on every other device.

Two other third-party nodes exist and are listed as evidence that the case is real rather than as endorsement: bitchat-esp32 and bitchat-relay. None has been tested against Airhop on hardware.

Deploying one

QuestionAnswer
Where?In the gap, and high. A relay is useful exactly where the chain breaks and nobody stands. Height clears the Fresnel zone and the ground bounce.
How many?One per gap. A ring of relays with no person between them works (scenario R04 proves each packet is forwarded once), and each still costs airtime, so more is not automatically better.
Bare or peer?Bare for a pure gap-filler. Peer if you want a permanent courier mailbox or gossip backfill on that corner.
Which band for the trunk?Whatever is legal where the node lives. 868 in Europe, 915 in North America. This is not a preference.
Which spreading factor?The lowest that reliably closes the link. Not the highest the radio offers.
Does it need internet?No. A relay is pure radio. A node with WiFi could additionally act as an internet gateway, which is a different feature.
Is it legal?BLE at 2.4 GHz, yes, on certified hardware. LoRa depends on band and duty cycle, which are region specific and enforced.
Can it read the traffic?No. Private messages are Noise-encrypted end to end, and a relay carries opaque ciphertext. Scenario R05 pins it: a private message survives a relay it cannot read.
Can it be seized?Yes, and a bare one holds nothing worth taking. A peer relay holds a Noise identity and possibly a courier bag of sealed envelopes, which is a real consideration for where you leave it.

Honest status

Simulated, never run against hardware

simulation/scenarios/relay.test.ts pins the forwarding rules against a relay that holds no keys and never announces: delivery through one relay (R01), discovery without the relay entering the roster (R02), a two-relay chain with no person between (R03), a three-relay ring forwarding each packet once (R04), and a private message crossing a node that cannot read it (R05). The 0xB1 handling is covered in the announce tests.

Neither has been run against hardware. A Bitle node in the room is the first thing worth checking when one is available, and it is a v1.3.0 roadmap item: Bitle firmware on the mesh with Noise XX, the courier mailbox, gossip sync and the 0xB1 flag read off a real announce; the LoRa trunk carrying traffic between two nodes with no phone bridging the gap; and the same runs against bitchat, so one deployed node serves both clients.

My assessment: the software side of this is in good shape, because the contract is so small. Forwarding requires no keys, no registry and no protocol knowledge beyond the header, which is why a third party could build a working relay from PROTOCOLS.md section 10 without reading Airhop's source. The risk is not in the protocol, it is in the hundred practical details of hardware that only appear when a box has been on a wall through a winter.

Drills

  • Why does a relay need no keys and no standing? Because forwarding consults no peer registry and verifies no signature. A relay is handed bytes, reads the header for the type and the TTL, decrements, dedupes on the packet ID, and puts them back on the air. Verification happens at the endpoints, and it must, because a relay routinely carries traffic for peers whose signing keys it has never seen. That is what lets a bare repeater extend the mesh with no change to the app and nothing on either phone knowing it is there.
  • Why is LoRa the right radio for a trunk and the wrong one for a phone? Its sensitivity is about -137 dBm at SF12 against BLE's -90 to -95, which is 40 dB, a factor of ten thousand in power, and sub-GHz free space loss is another 8 dB better. That buys kilometres. It costs bandwidth: 0.25 to 50 kbps, and airtime so long that European duty cycle rules allow roughly fourteen 50 byte messages an hour at SF12. That is fine for text between fixed nodes and useless for anything a phone does. And no phone has the radio.
  • Someone sets every node to SF12 for maximum range. What happens? The mesh chokes. A 50 byte packet occupies the channel for two and a half seconds, and in a mesh where every node repeats what it hears, the legal duty cycle budget is consumed forwarding other people's traffic before your own message goes out. The correct setting is the lowest spreading factor that reliably closes the specific link, not the highest the radio offers. Range and capacity are the same dial turned in opposite directions.
  • Is Meshtastic a competitor? No, it solves the opposite failure case. Meshtastic gives 2 to 15 km per hop and needs a dedicated device per person, so it is excellent in sparse outdoor terrain and impractical in a crowd where everyone would need hardware. Airhop needs no hardware and dies where nobody stands. A relay node carrying both radios is the bridge between the two regimes, which is exactly what Bitle is. Worth also noting the security difference: Meshtastic's default channel encryption is a shared symmetric key, which suits a hiking group and not strangers in a protest.
  • Why can the 0xB1 infrastructure flag never gate anything? Because a node signs its own announce, so the flag is inside signed bytes but is still a self-assertion any device can make. It is safe only because claiming it removes actions from your own row, which nobody gains from. The moment it gated a capability, a trust decision or a routing choice, it would become worth forging. Airhop reads bit 0 and never writes it, because a phone claiming to be infrastructure would hide its own Message button on every other device.
  • A relay is installed and messages seem to be dropped for the first few seconds. Is that a bug? No, and it is worth recognising because it reads exactly like one. A relay makes announces reachable, and delivery follows. A public message is verified against a signing key learned from an announce, so a message sent before that announce has propagated is dropped at the far end for being unverifiable. Once announces have crossed, everything works. The fix is patience, not code.
