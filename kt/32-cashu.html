In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Imagine a fairground where you swap real money for tokens at a booth. The clever part is how the booth stamps them: you put a blank token inside a sealed envelope with carbon paper, the booth stamps through the envelope without ever seeing the token, and you take the stamped token out. The booth knows it stamped one token, but not which one. So when you later hand that token to a friend and they bring it back, the booth cannot tell it came from you. And handing it to your friend needs no booth at all. It is just a piece of paper with a stamp on it.

Bitcoin and Lightning, in one section

Every Cashu balance is backed by bitcoin held over Lightning, so the two ends of the system need naming before the comparison below makes sense. No prior knowledge assumed.

Explain it to a ten year old

Bitcoin is a shared notebook that everyone in the world keeps a copy of. Writing in it is slow and costs money, because thousands of people have to agree on the entry. Lightning is what two people do when they expect to pay each other a lot: they write one entry that locks some money in a shared box, then keep score between themselves privately, and write a second entry only when they are done. If enough people have boxes with enough other people, a payment can hop across a chain of boxes to somebody you have never met.

The base layer

Bitcoin settles roughly every ten minutes, globally, with every full node verifying every transaction. That is what makes it hard to censor, and it is also why it cannot be a payment rail on its own: latency is minutes to hours, fees are set by an open fee market and can exceed the value of a small payment, and every transaction is public forever.

Routing, and the HTLC

A payment from someone with no channel to you hops across other people's channels. The problem is trusting the intermediaries with money in flight, and the answer is a hash time-locked contract. 

  1.  BOB picks a random PREIMAGE and hashes it
         payment_hash = SHA-256(preimage)
      and puts the hash in an invoice he gives ALICE

  2.  ALICE offers CAROL:  "pay out if you show me the preimage
                            for this hash, within N blocks"
      CAROL offers BOB the same, minus a fee

      ALICE ──HTLC──▶ CAROL ──HTLC──▶ BOB

  3.  BOB reveals the preimage to claim from CAROL
      CAROL now HAS the preimage, and claims from ALICE

      ALICE ◀──preimage── CAROL ◀──preimage── BOB

  Either the whole chain settles or all of it expires. Nobody can be
  left having paid out without being paid. The preimage is also the
  receipt: only Bob could have produced it.

A bolt11 invoice is the encoding of that request: an amount, the payment hash, an expiry, routing hints, and a signature from the recipient's node. It starts lnbc on mainnet, which is exactly what the wallet's invoice check tests for.

What Lightning costs, and why a phone cannot pay it

A Lightning node has to hold channels open, keep the funds inside them, and be online to receive. Being offline means being unable to accept a payment, and a channel is an on-chain transaction to open and another to close. None of that survives a phone in a pocket with no internet, which is the entire scenario Airhop is built for.

So the division of labour is: Lightning does the part that needs to be online and connected to the wider bitcoin economy, and ecash does the part that has to work in a field with no signal. The mint is the hinge, and what it costs you to use one is chapter 34.

Airhop uses both, in the roles each is good at: Cashu for transfer between people, Lightning to move value in and out through a mint. The deciding property is that a Cashu token is a string that carries value, so it flows over Bluetooth exactly like any other message, in a DM or even in a channel.

Blind signatures

David Chaum's 1982 idea, and the whole basis of the system. 

  THE CARBON PAPER ANALOGY

  1. You write a serial number on a piece of paper.
  2. You seal it in an envelope lined with CARBON PAPER.
  3. The bank stamps the OUTSIDE of the envelope.
     The stamp presses through the carbon onto the paper inside.
  4. You open the envelope. The paper is stamped.
     The bank has NEVER SEEN the serial number.

  Later you spend it. The bank sees the serial number for the first
  time, verifies its own stamp, and marks it spent.

  IT CANNOT LINK WHO WITHDREW IT TO WHO SPENT IT.

Two properties come out of this, and they are what make ecash different from a database entry at a payment company:

  • Unlinkability. The mint knows it issued N satoshis and later redeemed N satoshis, but cannot connect a specific issuance to a specific redemption. It cannot build a transaction graph.
  • Bearer. Possession is ownership. Nothing is registered to a person. That is also the risk: lose the string, lose the money, exactly like cash.

BDHKE, the actual construction

Cashu does not use RSA blind signatures. It uses Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange on secp256k1, which is smaller and fits the Bitcoin ecosystem's existing curve. 

  NOTATION
    G           the secp256k1 generator point
    k           the MINT's private key for one denomination
    K = kG      the mint's PUBLIC key for that denomination
    H(x)        hash_to_curve: maps a byte string to a curve point

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  MINTING

  ALICE                                        MINT
    x = 32 random bytes            (the secret)
    Y = H(x)                       (a point)
    r = random blinding factor
    B_ = Y + rG                    (BLINDED)
                        ──── B_ ────▶
                                                C_ = k * B_
                        ◀─── C_ ─────
    C = C_ - rK
      = k(Y + rG) - r(kG)
      = kY + krG - krG
      = kY                         ← the UNBLINDED signature over Y

    PROOF = (x, C)

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  REDEEMING

  ALICE                                        MINT
                        ──── (x, C) ───▶
                                                recompute Y = H(x)
                                                check  C == k * Y
                                                check  x not already spent
                                                mark x spent

  THE MINT SAW B_ AND C_ AT ISSUANCE.
  IT SEES x AND C AT REDEMPTION.
  r IS RANDOM AND SECRET, SO IT CANNOT CONNECT THE TWO.

Why denominations are powers of two

The mint has a separate key pair per denomination: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and so on. Any amount is a sum of powers of two, so 100 satoshis is 64 + 32 + 4, three proofs. This is why a wallet holds many small proofs rather than one balance, why "send exactly 100" usually works, and why the offline proof selector in cashu.ts notes that an exact subset usually exists.

It is also why a proof's amount must match a key the mint actually issues. A proof claiming a denomination the mint does not have is a forgery, and Airhop's DLEQ check treats a throw from the keyset lookup as exactly that rather than as an inconclusive result.

A token names exactly one mint. Ecash from two mints can never be combined into one token, which is why consolidateMints exists (chapter 33) and why balances are always shown per mint.

A decoding trap worth knowing

V4 tokens carry short keyset ids. Resolving them requires knowing the mint's keysets, and mapShortKeysetIds throws on any v2 short id (those beginning "01") when it cannot resolve them. Passing no keyset ids to the decoder is therefore not a neutral default: a perfectly good token from such a mint came back null and was reported to the user as unreadable.

NUT-00 requires the throw. An unresolved short id means the wallet does not know which keyset signed the proof, so it can neither verify it nor price its fee, and guessing would be worse than refusing. The fix is to supply what is already cached rather than to swallow the error, so callers pass the keysets of every mint the wallet knows.

DLEQ, and what it does not prove

NUT-12. A discrete log equivalence proof: the mint proves that the same private key k relates G to K and B_ to C_, without revealing k. 

  The mint proves:   log_G(K) == log_B_(C_)

  A Schnorr-style proof (e, s) attached to the proof.
  The recipient can verify it against the mint's cached PUBLIC keys,
  with NO NETWORK CONNECTION AT ALL.

This is the piece that makes offline receipt meaningful. Without it, a token handed to you in a dead zone is an unverifiable string. With it, you can confirm the mint really signed these proofs before the store ever sees them.

ResultMeaningAirhop's behaviour
validEvery witness present verified against cached keys. The mint definitely signed this.Accept, still stored as unverified until a mint round trip confirms it is unspent.
invalidA witness failed, or a proof claims a denomination the mint does not issue.Rejected before the store sees it. This is a forgery or corruption.
uncheckedNo witnesses present, or no cached keys for this mint or keyset.Reported as unchecked, never as valid.

DLEQ proves origin, not freshness

A valid witness proves the mint signed that proof. It cannot prove the sender has not already spent it. Only the mint knows that, and only over the network. This is the single most important limitation in the whole payment system and it is the reason offline-received proofs are stored as unverified and never claimed as confirmed.

The implementation detail that enforces honesty here: verifyTokenOffline returns unchecked when there is nothing to check against, rather than true. An implementation that returns true both when a witness is missing and when the check throws is one that can only ever say yes.

One subtlety in the check: require: false accepts a proof carrying no witness at all, which is the NUT-12 "MUST verify if present" rule. A mint that predates DLEQ is not a mint issuing bad proofs. Anything that does carry a witness must verify.

The NUTs Airhop implements

A NUT is a numbered Cashu specification, the equivalent of a NIP on the Nostr side. The client library is @cashu/cashu-ts, pinned at ^4.10.0 in package.json. That is the v4 API: Wallet, Mint and KeyChain, rather than the older CashuWallet and CashuMint pair.

NIPs, not NUTs

Two Nostr specifications sit alongside these and are easy to file in the wrong list. NIP-61 nutzaps are implemented: kind 10019 announces where and how to pay you, kind 9321 carries the payment itself. NIP-60, Nostr-hosted wallet state, is deliberately not implemented: publishing your proofs to a relay, even encrypted, puts your balance history somewhere you do not control. History stays on the device. Chapter 34.

The mint, and what you trust it with

The mint is the only trusted party in the payment system, and being precise about what that trust covers is the difference between a sensible product and a scam.

You DO trust the mint to

  • Honour redemption. It holds the backing bitcoin. It can refuse to pay, or run away with it.
  • Stay online. If it disappears, tokens issued by it are unspendable.
  • Not inflate. It could issue proofs it has no backing for.

You do NOT trust the mint with

  • Custody of your proofs. They are on your device, encrypted at rest.
  • Knowing your transaction graph. Blind signatures make issuance and redemption unlinkable.
  • Knowing who you paid. A transfer between two people never touches the mint.
  • Your identity. There is no account at a mint.
MitigationHow
The user chooses which mints to trustAirhop ships with no default mint. The user adds one, or runs their own.
One mint failing cannot take the restBalances are per (mint, unit) and never pooled. This is also why the balance screen cannot show one number.
Incoming ecash is only redeemed at a known mintA nutzap naming a mint the wallet does not already hold is refused outright, which is what NIP-61 assumes. This was a High severity finding.
Test mints are labelledA heuristic on the mint's own name, description and hostname (testnut, fakewallet, regtest, signet). Deliberately permissive, because a false positive costs a needless badge while a false negative lets someone mistake fake sats for real ones. It only ever adds a warning, never blocks.

The double-spend window, stated honestly

Offline, the sender can hand the same token to two people. Both copies are cryptographically valid. Whoever reaches the mint first keeps the value; the second redemption is refused.

Nothing can fix this without a network, because the only party who knows whether a proof is spent is the mint. So the design does the only honest thing available: an offline-received proof is stored as unverified and shown as such, never folded into the headline balance as settled money.

One consequence shows up in the offline proof selector, and it is a deliberate choice worth understanding. When selecting proofs to send, unverified proofs go first, largest first within each group. An unverified proof is the one most likely to be spent already, and the risk grows the longer it is held, so passing it on moves it toward a mint sooner than this wallet would reach one. The recipient is shown it as unverified rather than as settled. The usual defence, that they will swap immediately, does not apply in an app built for recipients with no signal.

That ordering is wrong for a melt, where the mint checks every input at once and an unverified one only raises the chance of failure, so payLightningInvoice selects with the library instead.

Token detection in messages

A payment is a message whose body contains a token string. The same channel or DM that carries text carries the payment, and the app renders it as a payment card. 

  Detection pattern, byte for byte the same as bitchat's:

    /\bcashu[AB][A-Za-z0-9._-]{40,}\b/g

  Bounds on hostile input:
    MAX_TOKEN_LENGTH       60,000 characters
    MAX_SCAN_LENGTH        120,000 characters
    MAX_TOKENS_PER_MESSAGE 3 cards
    MAX_AMOUNT             2.1e15, more sats than will ever exist

The pattern is deliberately identical to bitchat's, down to the character class and the 40-character minimum body. Both apps read the same messages off the same mesh, so a string that renders as a payment chip on one must render as a payment chip on the other. Being more permissive here would show a card where bitchat shows raw text.

One divergence: bitchat renders a generic chip for a V4 payload its minimal CBOR reader cannot walk. Airhop uses a full CBOR decoder, so a failure to decode means the token really is malformed, and no card is shown. Displaying a card for something that cannot be priced would be worse than showing the raw string.

Every displayed field from a token is sanitised, because it is attacker-controlled text sitting next to an amount: the unit must be alphanumeric and at most 12 characters or it falls back to sat, the mint host is lowercased and truncated to 48 characters, and the memo has control characters and newlines stripped (which would otherwise let a sender fake extra UI lines) and is capped at 80 characters.

A token in a public channel is money on a noticeboard

It is a bearer instrument. Anyone reading the channel can redeem it. The UI states this before sending, and it is not a bug: the same property that lets a token cross a dead zone in a DM makes it claimable by anyone who reads it in the open.

Alternatives

OptionVerdict
Lightning aloneRejected as the transfer mechanism. Routing happens live, so payment needs internet at the moment it is made, which is precisely the moment this app assumes there is none. Used as the rail in and out instead.
On-chain bitcoinRejected. Needs internet, confirmation times in tens of minutes, and fees that make small payments absurd.
FedimintThe closest relative: also Chaumian ecash, but federated across a group of guardians so no single party can run off with the backing. Genuinely better on the trust dimension. Heavier to run, less mature client tooling, and no bitchat compatibility. A reasonable future addition rather than a replacement.
A stablecoin or an L2 tokenRejected. Introduces a chain dependency, gas, an account model, and usually a compliance surface.
UPI or a bank railOn the roadmap as an online-only convenience, explicitly not a private rail. Every UPI transaction is KYC-linked and visible to the operator, which is the opposite of what this system is for. Cashu remains the offline payment system.
An IOU ledger with no mintRejected. Without a party who can be redeemed against, a token is a promise between strangers, and settling it needs exactly the coordination that is missing.

If you removed payments entirely

Airhop would lose its most distinctive feature and gain a considerably smaller attack surface. Payments bring a third-party library, a network dependency the rest of the app does not have, an encrypted storage partition, bearer secrets on disk, and an entire class of "did the money actually move" states the UI has to be honest about. It is the right feature for the product's mission, settling a bill in a dead zone is a real need, and it is also the part of the codebase where a bug costs the user money rather than a message.

Drills

  • Explain in one paragraph why a Cashu payment works with no internet. Because a token is a bearer instrument rather than a state update. The mint has already signed the proofs, blindly, at some earlier moment when there was a connection. Handing the string to someone else transfers the value, because value is possession of the string. No third party needs to be consulted, no route needs to be found, and no ledger needs updating at the moment of payment. What needs internet is putting value in, taking it out, and confirming a received token has not already been spent.
  • What exactly does a DLEQ proof tell you, and what does it not? It tells you the mint really signed this proof, verifiable offline against the mint's cached public keys. It tells you nothing about whether the proof has already been spent, because only the mint knows that and only over the network. So a DLEQ-valid token received in a dead zone is genuine and possibly worthless. That is why offline-received proofs are stored as unverified and never folded into the headline balance as settled money.
  • A sender hands the same token to two people offline. What happens? Both copies are cryptographically valid and both DLEQ-verify. Whoever reaches the mint first swaps successfully; the second is refused as already spent, and the wallet removes it from the balance rather than showing it as money. Nothing can prevent this without a network. The design's response is honesty rather than a fix: the unverified state exists precisely to represent "genuine, and not yet confirmed unspent".
  • Why does the wallet spend unverified proofs first? Because an unverified proof is the one most likely to already be spent, and the risk grows the longer it is held. Passing it on moves it toward a mint sooner than this wallet would reach one, and the recipient sees it labelled unverified rather than settled. The usual argument, that a recipient will swap immediately, does not hold in an app whose users may have no signal for days. The ordering is deliberately reversed for a melt, where the mint checks every input at once and an unverified one only raises the chance the whole operation fails.
  • Why are denominations powers of two? Because the mint holds one key pair per denomination, and powers of two let any amount be expressed as a small sum. 100 satoshis is 64 plus 32 plus 4, three proofs. That is why a wallet holds many small proofs rather than one balance, why exact offline sends usually succeed, and why a proof claiming an amount the mint does not issue is a forgery rather than an inconclusive case.
  • What is the strongest criticism of using Cashu here, and what is the answer? That it reintroduces a trusted third party into a product whose entire premise is having none. The answer has three parts. The trust is narrow and named: the mint can refuse redemption or inflate, and it cannot see your transaction graph, hold your proofs, or know who you paid. The user chooses the mint, and the app ships with none. And balances are per mint and never pooled, so one mint failing cannot take the rest. It is a real compromise, and the honest framing is that offline value transfer is not possible without one.
