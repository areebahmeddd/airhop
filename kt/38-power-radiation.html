Chapter 38 · The device

Battery, power and radiation

An app whose whole purpose is running in a pocket all day has one cost that dominates every other: how hard it drives the radio. This is the policy that decides that, the operating systems that fight it, and an honest answer to the question everybody eventually asks about keeping Bluetooth on all the time.

In plain words

Explain it to a ten year old

Listening for other phones is like shouting "anyone there?" and then holding your ear open for a reply. Doing that constantly, as loudly and as often as possible, wears you out. So the app has four speeds. Plugged in and you are looking at it: shout often. In your pocket: shout rarely, because nobody is waiting. Nearly out of battery: shout hardly at all. The trick is not the four speeds. It is that changing speed itself costs energy, so it refuses to change speed for a small wobble.

The problem

Airhop scans and advertises at the most aggressive settings the platform offers, LOW_LATENCY both ways with TX_POWER_HIGH. Originally it did so identically whether the app was on screen or pocketed, and whether the phone was at 100 percent or 4 percent.

The scale of it

Continuous LOW_LATENCY scanning is roughly an order of magnitude more expensive than LOW_POWER. For an app that is meant to sit in a pocket all day, that single setting is the largest battery cost the app has. Nothing else in the codebase is close.

The reason it matters more here than in a normal app is the mesh argument: a phone that dies at 4pm has stopped being a relay for everyone else, not just a client for its owner. Battery policy is therefore a network property, not only a courtesy.

Four power modes

src/services/power-policy.ts. Two inputs from the device, one from the app, and a pure function between them. 

  inputs                          modes
  ──────                          ─────
  batteryPercent  0..100 | null   performance        plugged in, on screen
  charging        bool            balanced           on battery, on screen
  appForeground   bool            power-saver        pocketed, or low battery
                                  ultra-low-power    critical battery


  bands, from the battery percentage

    normal     > 20 %
    low        <= 20 %          LOW_BATTERY_PERCENT
    critical   <= 10 %          CRITICAL_BATTERY_PERCENT


  modeFor(inputs, previousBand)          checked IN THIS ORDER

    1.  !appForeground   ->  critical ? ultra-low-power : power-saver
    2.  charging         ->  performance
    3.  band critical    ->  ultra-low-power
    4.  band low         ->  power-saver
    5.  otherwise        ->  balanced

Where the thresholds come from

The bands and the mode table follow bitchat-android's PowerProfileResolver in mesh/PowerManager.kt, deliberately. It is the reference implementation for this protocol, its thresholds are field-tested, and inventing different numbers would be guessing. Two things are Airhop's own: the hysteresis, and where the policy lives.

Hysteresis, and why it exists

bitchat re-resolves on every ACTION_BATTERY_CHANGED, which fires on every one percent step. A phone sitting at 20 percent while discharging, or wobbling either side of it while charging, flips modes repeatedly. Every flip restarts the scanner, which costs more than the mode change saves. 

  HYSTERESIS_PERCENT = 3,  and it is asymmetric on purpose

  Down is immediate. Up is sticky. Running too hard on a nearly
  flat phone is the failure that matters; running too gently for
  another 3 % of charge is not.

The other half of the mechanism is in PowerPolicy.next(): it returns null when nothing has changed, rather than returning the unchanged mode. That is what keeps a battery report crossing no boundary from restarting the scanner at all. A caller that ignored the difference would undo the whole optimisation.

reset() exists for one case: the radios going down. The next start then applies its mode explicitly rather than assuming native still holds the one it was last told.

The ordering is the policy

The five checks in modeFor are not interchangeable. Each position encodes a decision:

RulePositionWhy there
Background dominates everything First Off screen, nobody is waiting on discovery latency, so there is no reason to pay for it. This is also where a phone spends almost all of its day, so it is the branch that determines the daily cost.
Charging beats battery level Second Plugged in, the cost is somebody else's. A phone on a charger is the best relay in the room and should behave like one.
Critical before low Third and fourth Ordinary threshold ordering.
Foreground on battery is balanced, not performance Last A balanced scan still discovers peers in seconds, which is fast enough for somebody watching the Mesh tab. Reserving the most expensive setting for "plugged in" is what keeps the app from being the reason a phone dies.

A null battery reading is treated as healthy

batteryPercent is null until the platform reports. That resolves to the normal band, not to a cautious one. Refusing to scan properly because a percentage could not be read is a worse failure than the one it would avoid: it produces an app that finds nobody, on a device with a full battery, for a reason nothing on screen can explain.

Where the policy lives

In TypeScript, not in the native module, and the split is worth stating precisely. 

  TypeScript                        Native (Kotlin / Swift)
  ──────────                        ───────────────────────
  power-policy.ts                   observes the battery
    bandFor()                       reports the percentage
    modeFor()                       applies whichever mode
    PowerPolicy (hysteresis)          it is told

  radio-controller.ts               NO POLICY ON THAT SIDE
    owns "whether at all"
    owns "how hard"

Two reasons. The radios are already owned by one reconciler, and "how hard" belongs next to "whether at all" rather than in a different language. And it makes the decision a pure function, so the entire policy is exercised in Jest rather than on a device: you cannot easily test "what happens at 19 percent while charging" on real hardware, and you can trivially test it here.

The operating system fights back

Even a perfectly behaved app is throttled by the platform. The relevant mechanisms, and what Airhop does about each:

MechanismWhat it doesResponse
Doze (Android 6+) With the screen off and the device stationary, network access and wakelocks are deferred into maintenance windows. A foreground service with the connectedDevice type, which is exempt. It is also why the app shows a persistent notification.
App Standby buckets Rarely used apps are progressively restricted. The foreground service keeps the process in an active bucket while the mesh is running.
Scan throttling (Android 7+) Starting and stopping a scan more than five times in 30 seconds gets the scan silently ignored. The reconciler never restarts a scan it does not have to, which is precisely what the null return from PowerPolicy.next() protects.
Battery optimisation whitelist Apps not on it get more aggressive Doze treatment. The Mesh tab can send the user to the list. See below.
iOS background execution Backgrounded apps get a restricted CoreBluetooth mode: no scanning for a service UUID without specifying it, slower duty cycle, and advertising moves into an overflow area other iOS devices can see and Android usually cannot. Declared background modes, and the acceptance that iOS-to-Android in the background is weaker. Chapter 08.

The OEM problem

This is the part that no amount of correct code fixes. src/platform/battery-optimization.ts exists entirely because of it.

Aggressive skins reap foreground services

Xiaomi MIUI, Samsung One UI, Huawei EMUI, Oppo ColorOS and vivo Funtouch all kill foreground services that stock Android would leave alone. That breaks BLE scanning, the foreground service holding the process up, and the Nostr relay socket, all at once. The app cannot fix this from code. The only fix is the user whitelisting Airhop, and there is no single Android screen for that.

So the module answers a narrower question: where should this device be sent, as an ordered list of targets to try. The caller walks it and stops at the first one that opens. 

  resolveBatterySettingsTargets(os, brand)

    os !== "android"        ->  []          iOS has no equivalent concept

    brand matches an OEM    ->  [ { url: "miui://battery/autostart" },
    deep link                     { intent: IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATION_
                                             SETTINGS } ]

    otherwise               ->  [ { intent: IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATION_
                                             SETTINGS } ]

  The standard action is ALWAYS appended, never replaced. Every OEM
  scheme is undocumented and disappears between skin versions, so it
  is only ever the first thing tried.

Two lists, answering two different questions, and conflating them was the bug that produced the split:

ListQuestion it answersContents
AGGRESSIVE_OEMS Does this brand kill background services? Drives whether the Mesh tab says anything at all. xiaomi, redmi, poco, samsung, huawei, honor, oppo, realme, oneplus, vivo
OEM_DEEP_LINKS Does this brand expose a screen we can deep-link to? Only the MIUI, ColorOS and Funtouch families. Samsung's sleeping-apps screen lives inside com.samsung.android.lool and Huawei's protected-apps inside com.huawei.systemmanager, neither reachable by a public action, so both are in the first list only.

Two implementation traps, both recorded at the call site

A settings action is not a URL. React Native's Linking.openURL builds Intent(ACTION_VIEW, Uri.parse(url)) and never calls Intent.parseUri, so an intent:#Intent;action=...;end string resolves to nothing and throws. Linking.sendIntent(action) builds Intent(action) and pre-resolves it against the package manager, rejecting cleanly when nothing handles it. Hence a target that declares which of the two it is.

The list action, not the dialog. ACTION_REQUEST_IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATIONS pops a one-tap "allow?" dialog, but it needs the REQUEST_IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATIONS permission, which Google Play restricts to a short list of app categories Airhop is not in. Shipping it risks the store listing for a convenience. The list is one extra tap and no policy exposure.

iOS, which is a different animal

resolveBatterySettingsTargets returns an empty array on iOS, and that is correct rather than a stub. Background BLE on iOS is an entitlement declared in the bundle, not a per-app permission a user can revoke, so there is no screen to send anyone to. Low Power Mode still throttles, and the user can turn it off, but there is no Airhop-specific setting that would help.

The trade is real and runs the other way from Android. iOS gives less control and more predictability: no OEM skin is going to reap the process for reasons nobody documented.

Radiation: the honest answer

An app that asks a phone to transmit continuously, all day, in a pocket, invites a fair question. Here is the physics, then the numbers, then what is genuinely unknown.

Explain it to a ten year old

There are two kinds of "radiation". One kind, like X-rays, carries enough energy in each tiny packet to knock pieces off the atoms in your body. That is the dangerous kind. The other kind, like radio, light from a lamp, and the heat from your hand, does not have enough energy per packet to do that, no matter how much of it there is. Bluetooth is the second kind, and it is one of the weakest radios in the phone.

Ionising and non-ionising

The dividing line is a physical one, not a matter of degree. A photon ionises an atom only above roughly 10 electronvolts, which starts in the far ultraviolet. Everything below that, including all radio, can deposit energy as heat and nothing else.

The comparison that settles the practical question

A phone hunting for a weak cell tower transmits two to three orders of magnitude more power than BLE ever does, and every phone with a SIM does that already, all day, with no app installed. Running Airhop adds a radio that is a rounding error next to the one the phone was already using. If continuous BLE were a meaningful exposure, wireless earbuds, fitness trackers, car key fobs and every shop's beacon network would be a far larger one.

The regulatory frame

Exposure limits are set by ICNIRP internationally and by the FCC in the United States, expressed as Specific Absorption Rate, the watts of radio energy absorbed per kilogram of tissue. The limits are deliberately set well below the level at which measurable tissue heating occurs.

LimitValueWhere BLE sits
FCC, partial body, 1 g1.6 W/kgOrders of magnitude below. Devices are certified against their cellular and WiFi radios, which dominate.
ICNIRP, localised, 10 g2.0 W/kgSame.
WHO classification of RF fieldsGroup 2B, "possibly carcinogenic"The same category as pickled vegetables and aloe vera extract. It denotes limited evidence, not established risk.

Where honesty requires a hedge

The consensus position is that non-ionising radio at these power levels has no established health effect, and the mechanism by which it could have one is not known. That is not the same as proof of zero effect, and long-horizon epidemiology on any technology is genuinely hard. What can be said precisely is narrower and more useful: Airhop does not change the order of magnitude of anything. It adds the phone's weakest radio to a device already running its strongest. Anyone who wants to reduce exposure should look at the cellular modem and at holding the phone away from the head, not at Bluetooth.

The cost that is real

Battery, not biology. That is why this chapter is mostly about power policy: the measurable, defensible cost of running the mesh all day is charge, and the entire design above exists to bound it.

What has not been measured, and should be

There is no published battery-drain figure for Airhop: no milliamp-hours per hour per mode, on named handsets, over a measured period. The power policy is sound in structure and its thresholds come from a field-tested reference implementation, but "power-saver costs X percent per hour on a Pixel 8" is an empirical claim nobody in this repository has made. It belongs on the roadmap, and it is listed as a gap in chapter 49. Until it exists, treat every statement about relative cost here as a description of the design rather than as a measurement.

What breaks it

  • Re-resolving on every battery tick. The bug the hysteresis prevents. A phone at exactly 20 percent flips modes on every one percent report, and every flip restarts the scanner, which costs more than the mode change saves. Worse on Android 7 and above, where more than five scan restarts in 30 seconds gets the scan silently ignored.
  • Returning the unchanged mode instead of null. Structurally the same bug one layer up. The caller cannot tell "the answer is the same" from "apply this", so it applies it, and the scanner restarts anyway.
  • Treating a null battery reading as low. Produces a phone that finds nobody on a full charge, for a reason nothing on screen can explain.
  • An OEM deep link used as the only target. Every one of those schemes is undocumented and disappears between skin versions. Without the standard action appended behind it, a skin update silently leaves the user with a button that does nothing.
  • Posting a settings action through openURL. Throws, because React Native never parses an intent URI. The two dispatch paths are distinguished in the type for exactly this reason.
  • Shipping the one-tap battery dialog. Not a runtime failure but a distribution one: the permission it requires is Play-restricted to categories this app is not in.

If you removed it

The mesh would work perfectly and drain a phone in a few hours. The failure would be silent and would look like a hardware problem: a user who leaves the app running finds a flat phone by mid-afternoon, with no screen anywhere attributing it to the scan mode. Removing only the hysteresis would be subtler and arguably worse, because it produces a phone that scans less effectively at exactly the battery level where it is flipping modes fastest, and on Android 7 and above can get the scan throttled away entirely. Removing only the OEM module leaves the app working on stock Android and mysteriously dead on roughly half the handsets in the world.

Alternatives

ApproachHow it worksWhy not here
Policy in the native module Kotlin and Swift each decide their own mode from the battery they already observe Two implementations to keep in step, neither testable without a device, and "how hard to scan" separated from "whether to scan at all", which one reconciler already owns.
Continuous scaling Map the battery percentage onto a scan interval on a curve Every percentage change becomes a new interval and therefore a scanner restart. The discrete bands exist precisely so that most reports change nothing.
Learn the user's charging habits Scan harder when a charger is historically near A behavioural profile of when and where somebody charges their phone, held by an app whose threat model includes device seizure. The privacy cost is not worth a modest battery gain.
Let the user pick the mode A setting: aggressive, balanced, saver Reasonable as an addition and not as a replacement. Most users will never open it, and the ones who do cannot see the background and charging state that dominate the decision.
Ask for the one-tap battery exemption dialog ACTION_REQUEST_IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATIONS Better UX, Play-policy restricted to app categories this is not in. One extra tap is cheaper than a listing review.

Drills

  • A user says the app flattens their phone. What do you check, in order? First whether the mode is actually being applied: PowerPolicy.next() returns null when nothing changed, so a caller that treats null as "no mode" rather than "no change" would leave the radio at whatever it started on. Then whether the app is genuinely being backgrounded, since background is the first check in the resolver and dominates everything else, and an app kept alive by something else may never see the transition. Then charging state, because plugged in resolves to performance by design. Only after those does the band matter. And note the honest limit: there is no measured drain figure for any mode, so the answer at that point becomes an investigation rather than a lookup.
  • Why is the hysteresis one-directional? Because the two errors are not symmetric. Dropping into a lower band late means running the most expensive scan setting on a phone that is nearly flat, which is the failure that actually costs the user something. Climbing out of a lower band late means running a gentler scan for another three percent of charge, which costs almost nothing. So entering a band happens immediately at the threshold and leaving it needs the threshold plus three. Without any hysteresis, a phone parked at the threshold flips on every one percent report, and each flip restarts the scanner, which costs more than the mode change saves and on Android 7 and above can trip the five-restarts-per-30-seconds throttle that silently ignores the scan.
  • Someone asks whether keeping Bluetooth on all day is bad for them. Answer it without either dismissing them or overclaiming. Bluetooth is non-ionising radio: each photon carries roughly a millionth of the energy needed to break a chemical bond, so the only physical mechanism available to it is mild heating, and the regulatory limits are set well below where that becomes measurable. On magnitudes, BLE transmits about 1 to 10 milliwatts, while a phone hunting a weak cell tower transmits up to about 2 watts, two to three orders of magnitude more, and it does that already with no app installed. So Airhop does not change the order of magnitude of anything. The honest hedge is that no established health effect and no known mechanism is not the same as proof of zero effect, and long-horizon epidemiology is hard. Anyone wanting to reduce exposure should look at the cellular modem, not at Bluetooth. The cost that is genuinely real and genuinely caused by this app is battery.
  • Why does resolveBatterySettingsTargets always append the standard action instead of returning the OEM deep link alone? Because every one of those schemes is undocumented and disappears between skin versions. miui://battery/autostart is not an API, it is an observed behaviour of one settings app, and a MIUI update can remove it without anybody noticing. The standard IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATION_SETTINGS action exists on every Android 6 and later device including all of those skins, and it is where the whitelist actually lives. So the deep link is a shortcut to a better screen when it happens to work, and the standard action is the guarantee that the button always does something.
  • Why is a Samsung in the aggressive list but not in the deep-link list? Because those two lists answer different questions and conflating them was the bug that produced the split. "This brand kills background services" is about whether the Mesh tab should say anything at all, and Samsung does kill them. "This brand exposes a screen we can deep-link to" is about whether a shortcut exists, and Samsung's sleeping-apps screen lives inside com.samsung.android.lool with no public action pointing at it. Same for Huawei's protected-apps inside com.huawei.systemmanager. Both belong in the first list and neither in the second; they get the standard battery-optimisation list, which is where the whitelist lives on those devices too.
  • Why is the power policy a pure function rather than a method on the radio controller? Because the interesting cases are almost impossible to produce on a device and trivial to produce in a test. "What happens at 19 percent while charging with the app backgrounded" needs a specific phone at a specific charge in a specific state; as a pure function it is one line of Jest. The hysteresis in particular has a stateful component, which is why PowerPolicy is a small class that remembers the previous band, but the decision itself, bandFor and modeFor, stays pure and takes the previous band as an argument. Native keeps only mechanism: observe the battery, report it, apply whichever mode it is told, no policy on that side.
